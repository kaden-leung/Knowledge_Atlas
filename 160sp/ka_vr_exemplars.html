Eight studies worth imitating

The set is curated to span the range of design types — within-subjects and between-subjects, behavioural and self-report and autonomic-with-sensor — without overwhelming. Each entry carries the design, the construct, and the one or two features a 160 Fall student should imitate. The point is not "this is a great paper" but "here is the specific, copyable thing it does well."

Riva et al. (2007)

Within-subjectsSelf-report

Affective space in immersive VR. Within-subjects design, N = 36; manipulated the affective qualities of the same architectural space (warm/cool, open/closed) while measuring mood and presence.

Why it's a role model: single-variable manipulation on one base scene, a well-validated mood measure (PANAS), and clean within-subjects controls — exactly the discipline a short project needs.

Slater et al. (2010)

Between-subjectsSelf-report + behavioural

Body-ownership illusion in VR. Between-subjects design, N = 40; manipulated the perspective from which participants saw a virtual body and measured ownership ratings.

Why it's a role model: a falsifiable, theory-driven prediction stated in advance, plus multiple converging measures (self-report ratings paired with behavioural response) so the construct claim does not rest on one method.

Smaldino-style pre-registered example

Pre-registeredSingle primary outcome

A small-N pre-registered VR study. Pre-registered, N = 48, a single primary outcome, with the predicted direction stated in advance of data collection.

Why it's a role model: the student-relevant features — small N, single outcome, pre-registration, honest reporting — are exactly what a 7-to-10-week project can match. It proves modest scope and rigour are compatible.

Slater et al. (2006) — virtual Milgram

Within / mixedBehavioural + autonomic

A virtual re-enactment of Milgram's obedience paradigm. Participants administered "shocks" to a virtual learner; the design compared a visible-and-audible avatar against a text-only condition, with skin-conductance and heart-rate recorded.

Why it's a role model: it shows how to study an ethically impossible real-world phenomenon in VR, and pairs a behavioural index with autonomic measures so "stress" is operationalised, not assumed.

Meehan et al. (2002) — VR pit room

Within-subjectsAutonomic-with-sensor

Physiological reactivity to a virtual height threat. A within-subjects design contrasted a high-anxiety "pit" room with a low-anxiety training room, recording heart rate, skin conductance, and skin temperature as presence proxies.

Why it's a role model: it validates an objective measure (heart-rate change) against the construct it is meant to index, rather than relying on self-reported presence alone — a model for picking a defensible DV.

Bailenson et al. (2003) — interpersonal distance

Mixed factorialBehavioural

Proxemic behaviour toward virtual humans. A factorial design crossed avatar gaze (mutual vs averted) with agency belief, measuring the physical distance participants kept from the virtual human — an unobtrusive behavioural DV.

Why it's a role model: it uses a clean, automatically logged behavioural measure (tracked distance) that needs no rater, and shows how a tightly factorial design isolates one factor at a time.

Loomis, Blascovich & Beall (1999)

Methods exemplarTool paper

Immersive virtual environment technology as a basic research tool. A foundational methods paper laying out why VR offers experimental control plus ecological realism, and how to operationalise constructs inside it.

Why it's a role model: it is the reference for justifying why VR is the right method for a question — the exact argument your Week-1 rationale paragraph has to make.

Won, Bailenson et al. (2015) — homuncular flexibility

Within-subjectsBehavioural performance

Adaptation to a remapped virtual body. A within-subjects design measured how quickly participants learned to control a virtual body whose limbs were remapped to novel movements, using task-completion time as the performance DV.

Why it's a role model: a single, surprising, falsifiable hypothesis tested with one objective performance measure across a manageable sample — a clean template for a focused class project.

How to use this set

Do not try to imitate a whole study. Pick the one feature from one of these exemplars that fits your question — a single-variable manipulation on one base scene, two converging measures, an automatically logged behavioural DV, a pre-registered single outcome — and build your Week-1 topic around carrying that feature through. When you write your rationale on the deliverable page, you should be able to name which exemplar your design takes after and why.

Design rationale

Eight is the target count — enough to span within-subjects vs between-subjects, and behavioural vs self-report vs autonomic-with-sensor, without overwhelming a Week-1 reader. Each entry deliberately ends with a "Why it's a role model" line that names a specific, imitable feature rather than praising the paper in general — "single-variable manipulation, validated mood measure" rather than "Riva is a great paper."

Dependencies

None operational — the page is reference content with no corpus binding. The only outstanding dependency is editorial: instructor approval of the curated set and a check of the five newly composed entries' design descriptions against the source papers.
