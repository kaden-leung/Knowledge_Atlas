# Four Readers on the Web of Belief

*David Kirsh, 2026-05-19. A simulated panel reading of `docs/EN_OVER_BN_WEB_OF_BELIEF_THINKING_2026-05-19.md`. Paul Thagard, Susan Haack, and Nancy Cartwright each read the EN-over-BN essay, elaborate it, sharpen its analysis, and add to it; Judea Pearl then enters as critic; the three address his objections. As with the VOI panel materials, the four voices below are **CW simulations** reconstructed from each thinker's published work, not transcripts of real consultation. They are labelled as simulation throughout and should be read as "the strongest version of the position each thinker's corpus would license," not as anything they have said. A real-panel transmission, were DK to want one, would follow the VOI-panel precedent.*

---

## How to read this document

The EN-over-BN essay argued that an Epistemic Network — a web of warranted belief — sitting on top of a Bayesian network reveals a definite class of things about a scientific field that neither a BN alone nor a retrieval-augmented language model can reveal. This document stress-tests that argument. Sections 2–4 are constructive: three philosophers of science whose life's work bears directly on the essay's claims read it and make it better. Section 5 consolidates what they added. Section 6 is adversarial: Judea Pearl, whose Bayesian-network and causal-inference programme the essay leaned on and partly set itself against, raises seven objections. Section 7 is the philosophers' reply. Section 8 states where the exchange leaves the design.

The exercise is not ornamental. The EN-over-BN architecture is a wager (the essay said so in its §10), and a wager is worth more after it has been argued against by the people most equipped to argue.

## 2. Paul Thagard reads the essay

*Simulated. Reconstructed from Thagard's work on explanatory coherence and the ECHO model (Thagard, 1989, 2000), conceptual revolutions (Thagard, 1992), emotional cognition (Thagard, 2006), and the cognitive science of science (Thagard, 2012).*

I am sympathetic to this essay almost to the point of partisanship, because its upper layer is the thing I have spent forty years arguing scientific cognition actually runs on. But sympathy is not much use to a draft. Let me say where it is thin and what it should add.

The essay treats coherence as if it were mostly the absence of contradiction — it speaks of a "tension count" and of contradictions resolved. That is the weakest possible reading of coherence and it undersells the upper layer badly. Coherence is not contradiction-counting; it is *constraint satisfaction*. In the account I have defended since 1989, a belief network has positive constraints and negative constraints, and coherence is the global partition of beliefs into accepted and rejected that maximises satisfied constraint weight. The negative constraints are contradiction and competition — two hypotheses that explain the same evidence compete even if they do not strictly contradict. But the positive constraints are the ones the essay omits, and they are where the action is: a hypothesis coheres with what it *explains*, and with what explains *it*, and with hypotheses it stands in *analogy* to. An Epistemic Network whose edges are only "support" and "contradict" is a coherence network with its explanatory heart cut out. The EN's edges should be typed: *explains*, *is-explained-by*, *is-analogous-to*, *contradicts*, *competes-with*. Then the coherence score is computed, not counted, and it means something.

This matters for the essay's own examples. Take a case the essay would profit from: the *Helicobacter pylori* theory of peptic ulcers. When Marshall and Warren proposed in the early 1980s that a bacterium, not stress and acid, caused ulcers, the finding had respectable warrant — they had the organism, they had the association, Marshall eventually infected himself. Yet the field disbelieved it for a decade. Why? Not because the *d* and the *p* were bad. Because the bacterial hypothesis cohered badly with an entrenched explanatory structure — "no bacterium survives the stomach's acid" — and cohered badly with the therapeutic and commercial structure built around acid-suppression. An Epistemic Network that typed its edges as *explanatory* would have shown exactly this: a finding with good data-priority warrant but low explanatory coherence with the surrounding web. The essay's "tension count" would have registered a tension; my ECHO network would have shown *where* the tension was and *what kind* it was — and would have flagged that the field faced a choice between rejecting a well-warranted datum and reorganising an entrenched explanatory structure. That is a more useful diagnosis, and it is available only if coherence is computed over typed explanatory relations.

Second, and more fundamental: the essay assumes the *concepts* stay fixed while the *credences* move. Every node is a belief, and beliefs gain and lose warrant. But the deepest scientific change is not credence change — it is *conceptual* change. In a conceptual revolution the kind-hierarchy itself reorganises: "whale" jumps from the fish branch to the mammal branch; "heat" stops being a substance and becomes molecular motion; "ulcer" moves from the stress-disease branch to the infectious-disease branch. The credences do not merely shift; the nodes are *redrawn*. An EN that can only re-weight a fixed graph will represent the easy 90% of scientific change and miss the 10% that matters most. The design implication is concrete: the EN needs a representation of the concept hierarchy *distinct from* the belief graph, and it needs to detect when a finding is pressing not on a belief but on a category boundary. The Atlas's "jingle-jangle" proliferation warnings are a primitive start on this — they notice when one word covers two concepts — but they are a start, not the thing.

Third, the essay's notion of entrenchment is too cold. It treats entrenchment as a structural fact — central nodes are expensive to revise. True, but incomplete. Entrenchment also has an *affective* dimension. Scientists do not merely find it logistically costly to give up a central theory; they are attached to it, and the attachment is itself part of the cognitive dynamics. I have called this emotional or "hot" coherence: beliefs carry valence, and valence participates in the constraint satisfaction. For a corpus meant to *survey a field honestly*, this is not a curiosity. A theory can be entrenched because it is well-warranted, or entrenched because it is beloved, and those are different states with different prognoses. The EN should distinguish *evidential* entrenchment from *affective* entrenchment, because a field whose central theories are affectively but not evidentially entrenched is a field ripe for a fall, and that is precisely the kind of thing a survey should be able to say.

One addition the essay should simply make. It is right that the BN cannot represent incoherence and the EN can. It should go further: the EN can represent incoherence *and compute its resolution*. ECHO is an algorithm; it takes a network of typed constraints and returns an acceptance partition. Pearl will demand a calculus — I can hear it coming — and the answer is that the EN has one. It is not the probability calculus. It is a coherence calculus, and it has been a working, implemented, formal algorithm since 1989.

## 3. Susan Haack reads the essay

*Simulated. Reconstructed from Haack's foundherentism and the crossword analogy (Haack, 1993), her account of the degrees and dimensions of evidential quality, her defence of inquiry against both scientism and cynicism (Haack, 2003), and her Peircean strictures on genuine versus sham inquiry (Haack, 1998).*

The essay is a good essay and I shall now make trouble for it, because that is what a good essay deserves.

My first trouble is with its Quinean frame. The essay calls the upper layer a "web of belief" and means Quine. But Quine's web is, for my taste, too purely coherentist: in his picture the web meets experience only at its edges, and revision is governed by conservatism and simplicity within the web. That cannot be the whole story, and a corpus that took it for the whole story would license a disaster. A coherent set of beliefs can be a coherent fairy tale. Coherence among the entries is necessary and is not sufficient. My own picture — I have called it foundherentism, an ugly word for a sensible position — is the crossword puzzle. How reasonable a crossword entry is depends on two things, not one: on how well it is supported by its *clues* (this is the analogue of experiential evidence, the foundationalist's insight, which pure coherentism discards) and on how well it fits with the *intersecting entries already filled in* (this is coherence, the coherentist's insight, which pure foundationalism discards). The Epistemic Network, if it is to be more than a beautiful fairy tale, must have *clues* as well as *entries*. The essay's warrant decomposition has mechanism warrant and entrenchment warrant and coherence-with-theory — those are intersecting entries. Where in the decomposition are the *clues*? The empirical findings, anchored as directly as findings ever are to observation, must enter the EN with a distinct status — not incorrigible, nothing is incorrigible, but *differently grounded*. Thagard's "data priority" principle is the same instinct. The EN needs it explicitly, or it is coherentism with extra steps.

My second trouble, and this one is sharp, is with the essay's flirtation with probability. The essay is right that the BN's single number conflates balance and weight. But it does not draw the full moral, which is this: **warrant is not a probability at all, and does not obey the probability calculus.** Consider. If warrant were a probability, the warrant of a claim C and the warrant of its negation not-C would sum to one. They do no such thing. Evidence can fail to warrant C without thereby warranting not-C — it can simply be inconclusive, leaving *both* poorly warranted. The probability calculus cannot represent that; degrees of warrant can, easily, because warrant has a dimension the probability calculus lacks. I distinguish three determinants of how good someone's evidence for a belief is. First, *supportiveness* — how strongly the evidence bears on the claim; this is roughly the essay's "balance," and it is the only one a probability captures. Second, *comprehensiveness* — how much of the relevant evidence the evidence includes; this is the essay's "weight," and Keynes is quite right that a probability cannot carry it. But there is a third, which the essay misses entirely and which I will give it: *independent security* — how warranted the supporting evidence is, *independently of the claim it is being used to support*. A finding may be well supported by evidence E; but if E is itself believed largely because of the very claim it supports, the warrant is circular and worth little. The EN must represent independent security, because scientific literatures are *full* of quiet circularity — a self-report instrument validated against a theory and then used to confirm the theory; a measure whose construct validity rests on its agreement with the finding it later certifies. A warrant decomposition with supportiveness and comprehensiveness but not independent security will systematically over-rate co-validated bodies of work, and co-validated bodies of work are exactly where fields fool themselves.

My third trouble is that the essay assumes the corpus is full of *inquiry*. Much of what is published is not inquiry. Inquiry, in Peirce's sense and mine, is the genuine attempt to find out how things are, conducted by someone willing to be wrong. A great deal of published work is instead what Peirce called sham reasoning and what I have called pseudo-inquiry — the marshalling of considerations toward a conclusion already fixed, whether fixed by ideology, by commercial interest, or by the simple wish to have been right. A finding produced by pseudo-inquiry can have a perfect *d* and *p*. The EN, if it is to survey a field's warrant honestly, needs a channel — even a coarse one — for the genuineness of the inquiry that produced a finding: pre-registration, declared interests, whether the design could have embarrassed the authors. This is not cynicism about science. I am the reverse of a cynic about science. It is the recognition that the warrant of a finding depends on the *attitude* of the inquiry that produced it, and that a corpus which cannot tell genuine inquiry from advocacy will quietly inherit the literature's motivated reasoning under the dignified name of "evidence."

I will end with praise, since the essay has earned it. Its insistence that the credence be an *audit trail one can argue with* is exactly right and is the whole point. A warrant you cannot interrogate is not a warrant; it is an oracle. Keep that. Build everything else around it.

## 4. Nancy Cartwright reads the essay

*Simulated. Reconstructed from Cartwright's work on the disunity of science and the dappled world (Cartwright, 1999), capacities and nomological machines (Cartwright, 1989), the limits of probabilistic causality (Cartwright, 1979), and the use of evidence for policy (Cartwright & Hardie, 2012).*

The essay's §7 on scope and the travelling of claims is the part nearest my own work, and I am going to tell it that it does not go nearly far enough.

The essay says, correctly, that a result is established first for the narrow set-up that produced it, and that its credence for a wider claim is a separate and smaller thing — it has the Atlas's three-credence decomposition, as-tested then scope-adjusted then network. Smaller credence for the wider claim. But "smaller credence for the same claim" is the wrong picture. When a finding travels from the laboratory to the world it does not become *the same claim held less confidently*. It becomes *a different claim*. The laboratory result is a claim about what happens inside a particular arrangement of apparatus, controls, and population — what I have called a *nomological machine*: a stable configuration of components whose operation, shielded from interference, gives rise to a regularity. The regularity is the output of the machine. Take the machine away and you do not have the regularity held with less confidence; you have no warrant for a regularity at all, because regularities are not free-standing — they are what nomological machines produce. So the EN's three credences are better than one credence, but they should not be three credences on one node. The as-tested finding and the scope-adjusted claim are *two nodes*, joined by an inference that itself needs warrant — and the warrant of that inference is a separate object the EN must carry.

What licenses the inference from the machine's output to a claim about the world? Not more confidence. A separate argument, about *capacities*. This is the second thing the essay should add. Underneath the finding "nature views speed cognitive recovery" is not, fundamentally, a regularity; it is a *capacity* — nature views have the capacity to support attentional restoration, a capacity they carry from setting to setting even though the *regularity* they produce varies wildly with what else is present. Capacities travel; regularities do not. If the EN had capacity nodes beneath its finding nodes, it could represent the thing that actually licenses generalisation: not "the effect was 0.45 in the lab, discount to 0.30 for the world," but "the capacity is real and stable; whether it manifests as a usable effect in setting S depends on the support factors present in S." The Atlas already gropes toward this with its mechanism nodes. A mechanism is close to a capacity. The essay should say so and lean on it.

Third, the matter of evidence and what it can do. In the policy book Hardie and I distinguished evidence that *clinches* a conclusion from evidence that merely *vouches* for it. A well-conducted randomised trial clinches — deductively, given its assumptions — a very narrow conclusion: that the treatment caused the average effect *in that trial population*. It vouches for nothing wider. The wider claim — it will work here, in this school, this clinic, this building — is not clinched by the trial; it requires a further argument using causal-principle knowledge and a survey of whether the support factors the effect needs are present in the target. The essay's epistemic VOI should be told this, because it changes what a high-VOI study is. A replication that clinches the same narrow conclusion a third time has low epistemic VOI. A study that establishes a *support factor* — that tells you *which* feature of the setting the effect needs in order to travel — has high epistemic VOI, because it is what converts a clinched-but-local result into a usable general one. VOI computed over warrant should reward support-factor studies specifically.

And now a warning, which puts me at odds with Thagard and the essay should hear the disagreement rather than have it smoothed over. The essay, and Thagard even more, treat rising coherence as the goal — the field should tend toward an integrated, low-tension web. I do not believe the world is built so that this is the right aim. The world is dappled. It is a patchwork of regimes in which different models, none of them universal, each work in their own domain and do not integrate. A field can be in excellent epistemic health and *not* be coherent in the strong sense — it can rightly contain several models that work in their own patches and resist unification, because unification is not available. An EN that scores low coherence as a defect, and that runs an ECHO-style maximiser toward a single accepted partition, will pressure a field toward a false unity and will mislabel honest disunity as tension to be resolved. The coherence score is a real and useful instrument. But it should be read as a *diagnostic*, never as an *objective function*. The essay's §10 caution should be strengthened to say so explicitly: do not maximise coherence; measure it, and ask, each time it is low, whether the right response is to resolve the tension or to accept that this corner of the world is dappled and the models are not meant to meet.

## 5. What the three readers have added

Before the critic enters, it is worth consolidating, because the essay is now materially stronger and the strengthening is concrete.

From Thagard: coherence must be *computed over typed explanatory relations* (explains, is-explained-by, is-analogous-to, contradicts, competes-with), not counted as contradictions; the EN needs a *concept hierarchy distinct from the belief graph* so that conceptual revolutions — category-boundary moves — are representable and not just credence shifts; entrenchment must be split into *evidential* and *affective* components, because a field whose central theories are loved but not warranted is a field about to fall; and the EN does have a calculus — a coherence calculus, ECHO — which answers the demand for formality without being the probability calculus.

From Haack: the EN must have *clues as well as entries* — empirical findings entering with a distinct, anchored (not incorrigible) status, or the web is coherentism with extra steps; warrant is *not a probability* and does not obey its calculus, because the warrant of C and of not-C do not sum to one; warrant has a *third dimension the essay missed* — independent security, the non-circularity of the supporting evidence — and without it the EN will over-rate quietly co-validated literatures; and the EN needs a channel for the *genuineness of inquiry*, because a finding from pseudo-inquiry can have a flawless *d* and *p*.

From Cartwright: a travelling claim is *a new claim, not the same claim held less firmly* — the as-tested and scope-adjusted credences should be two nodes joined by an inference that itself needs warrant; the EN should carry *capacity nodes* beneath finding nodes, because capacities travel and regularities do not; epistemic VOI should specifically reward *support-factor studies*, since those are what make a clinched-but-local result usable; and coherence must be a *diagnostic and never an objective function*, because the world is dappled and forced unity is a falsification.

That is a sharper essay than the one they were handed. Now the hard part.

## 6. Judea Pearl's critique

*Simulated. Reconstructed from Pearl's work on Bayesian networks (Pearl, 1988), the causal hierarchy and do-calculus (Pearl, 2009; Pearl & Mackenzie, 2018), and transportability theory (Bareinboim & Pearl, 2016).*

I have read the essay and the three elaborations, and I will be blunt, because politeness has never advanced a science. There is something here, but it is dressed in a confusion, and the confusion is worth more than the dress.

*One. You have built a straw Bayesian network and then defeated it.* The "BN" of the essay is a network of associations — rung one of the ladder of causation. Of course rung one cannot represent confounding; rung one cannot represent anything causal at all. But I did not spend forty years on rung one. A causal Bayesian network represents interventions (rung two) and counterfactuals (rung three). The essay's central dramatic claim — that the BN cannot represent the suspicion of an unmodelled confound — is simply false of the framework I actually built. An unmeasured common cause is represented, explicitly, as a bidirected edge in a semi-Markovian model. It is not unrepresentable; it is a standard object.

*Two. Identifiability already does what your "warrant" gestures at.* When you suspect confounding, you do not need a new epistemic layer. You ask whether the causal effect is *identifiable* given the graph — and the do-calculus answers, completely and decidably, by the ID algorithm. If the effect is identifiable, the confound does not threaten it. If it is not, you know precisely that, and you know which assumptions would rescue it. That is not woolly "warrant." That is a theorem.

*Three. Your "doubt about the graph itself" is a hyperprior.* The essay says a BN cannot represent a doubt about its own structure because the structure is its frame. This is out of date by thirty years. Put a prior over graph structures. Do Bayesian model averaging. The doubt about the structure is a distribution over structures, and it updates by Bayes' rule like everything else. Hierarchical Bayesian modelling absorbs the entire upper layer — study quality becomes a latent node, framework plausibility becomes a hyperparameter, and your "Epistemic Network" becomes a Bayesian model with more levels.

*Four. "Warrant" and "coherence" have no calculus, and what has no calculus cannot be trusted.* Thagard says ECHO is a calculus. ECHO is a constraint-satisfaction heuristic with hand-set weights; it is not a calculus in the sense that the do-calculus is a calculus — sound, complete, with a semantics. Haack says warrant is not a probability. Then what is its algebra? "It has three dimensions" is not an algebra. An epistemology without a calculus is a mood.

*Five. Coherence is not truth, and you have made coherence load-bearing.* A maximally coherent web can be maximally wrong. Ptolemaic astronomy was gorgeously coherent. You have built an instrument that rewards a field for agreeing with itself. The history of science is the history of coherent systems overturned by one stubborn, incoherent, *correct* data point. An instrument that scores coherence will be conservative exactly when science needs to be radical.

*Six. You have abandoned the discipline of causation for the comfort of belief-talk.* The hard problem is getting the causal structure of the world *right*. Your EN is about how a field *feels* about its beliefs — entrenchment, tension, warrant. A field can feel wonderful about a false causal model. I would rather have the right model and a field in turmoil than a coherent field and the wrong model.

*Seven. Data is dumb, and so, I am afraid, is consensus — but causal assumptions are not negotiable by panel.* You propose to survey a field by modelling its web of belief. But the causal assumptions that actually matter are true or false regardless of how the web feels about them. An ounce of correct causal assumption outweighs a ton of coherent belief. Model the causation. The sociology of the field is not the epistemology of the field.

## 7. The three readers answer Pearl

**Cartwright, on the straw-BN charge and on causation.** Judea is right that we must not caricature the causal network, and the essay should credit rungs two and three explicitly. But the charge misfires, because the causal hierarchy and the EN are not answering the same question. The do-calculus tells you whether an effect is identifiable *given the graph*. Given the graph. Every theorem Judea has, and they are real theorems, is conditional on the causal assumptions encoded in the graph and in the selection diagram. The do-calculus consumes those assumptions; it does not produce them, and Judea has said so himself more plainly than anyone — data is dumb, the causal assumptions must come from outside. The EN is a model of that outside. It is the layer that asks: *this graph, rather than that one — how well warranted is it? which rival graph does the field have most reason to fear? what would it cost to give up the assumption the identification result rests on?* Judea's framework brackets that question by design and calls it "expert knowledge." The EN unbrackets it. Far from abandoning causal discipline, the EN supplies causal modelling with the one thing the do-calculus explicitly cannot: an account of the warrant of its own premises. And on transportability — Judea's selection diagrams are the formal partner of my travelling-of-claims, and the essay should say so warmly. But a selection diagram must be *posited*: someone must judge which differences between source and target are causally relevant. That judgement is exactly an EN act. Transportability calculus and the EN are not rivals. One needs the other.

**Haack, on the hyperprior and the demand for a calculus.** Judea's third objection — that doubt about the structure is just a hyperprior over structures — is the serious one, and it fails for two reasons. First, a prior over structures requires an enumerated space of structures. The structures that matter most are the ones *no one has yet conceived* — the history of science is very largely the history of the unconceived alternative becoming conceived. You cannot put a proper prior over a hypothesis you have not formulated, and so Bayesian model averaging quietly assumes away precisely the doubt it claims to model. Second, and this answers the demand for a calculus as well: a hierarchical Bayesian model, however many levels you stack, returns at the top a *probability*. And warrant is not a probability — this is not a mood, Judea, it is an argument, and here it is. If warrant were a probability, the warrant of C and of not-C would sum to one. They do not: inconclusive evidence leaves both unwarranted. No amount of hierarchy repairs this, because every level of the hierarchy still outputs a quantity obeying the probability axioms, and the phenomenon to be captured — comprehensiveness, the *weight*, how much of the evidence is in — violates those axioms. Warrant *does* have an algebra. It is not the probability algebra. It is closer to the logic of defeasible argumentation — Pollock's, made formal — in which support and defeat are tracked separately and do not net to a single complemented number. That a calculus is not *your* calculus does not make it a mood.

**Thagard, on coherence, conservatism, and the stubborn data point.** Judea's fifth and sixth objections are the ones I must answer, because they are aimed at me. He says a coherent web can be coherently wrong — Ptolemy — and that an instrument scoring coherence will be conservative when science must be radical. Both halves are mistaken, and the mistake is the same: he is imagining pure coherentism, and neither the essay nor I have ever defended it. Explanatory coherence has, as a *constitutive principle*, data priority — empirical findings carry a privileged, though not incorrigible, independent weight. This is exactly Haack's "clues." A coherent web that must reject a well-warranted, independently secure data point pays a coherence penalty for doing so; the instrument does not reward the agreement, it registers the strain. That is not conservatism — it is the precise representation of the moment before a revolution. Ptolemaic astronomy did not fall because someone disliked its coherence; it fell because the data-priority constraints from precise observation accumulated until the coherence-maximising partition *flipped* to Copernicus. ECHO models exactly that flip; I have published the simulation. The "stubborn incoherent correct data point" Judea celebrates is, in my framework, a node with high data-priority warrant and low coherence with the entrenched theory — and the *whole value* of the EN is that it makes that node *visible and findable* instead of letting the field bury it. Judea wants the right model and a field in turmoil. So do I. The EN is the instrument that finds the turmoil-causing datum and puts it on the dashboard. It is the opposite of a conservatism engine. And on the calculus: the do-calculus is sound and complete for identifiability, and I admire it without reservation. It is also silent about every question the EN exists to answer. Soundness and completeness are virtues relative to a question. For "is this effect identifiable given the graph," take Judea's calculus. For "how should the field apportion belief across rival graphs, given evidence of varying weight, security, and genuineness," the calculus is constraint satisfaction over typed relations, and it is no less formal for being computed by relaxation rather than by derivation.

**Cartwright, closing, on the seventh objection.** Judea says the sociology of the field is not the epistemology of the field, and that we have confused them. We have not, but the essay must be worded so that no one could think we had. The EN does not measure how the field *feels*; entrenchment in the EN is not popularity. It measures structural and evidential facts: how much else depends on a belief, how much independently secure evidence stands under it, how far its supporting claims have been shown to travel. Affective entrenchment — Paul's "hot" coherence — is tracked *precisely so that it can be held apart from* and contrasted with evidential entrenchment, so that the instrument can say "this theory is loved more than it is warranted." That is not doing sociology instead of epistemology. That is doing the epistemology of a field as it is actually conducted by human beings, which is the only way it is ever conducted. Judea wants the right causal model. So do we. We are telling him something he himself insists on and then forgets: the right causal model is chosen, always, against a field of rivals, on evidence of uneven quality, by people with commitments — and the discipline of *that* choice is not in the do-calculus. It cannot be. The do-calculus starts after the choice is made. The EN is the discipline of the choice.

## 8. Where the exchange leaves the design

The essay went into the room a wager and came out a better-specified wager. The specification now includes, as concrete design obligations: typed explanatory edges and a computed coherence rather than a counted one; a concept hierarchy held separately from the belief graph, so conceptual revolution is representable; entrenchment split into evidential and affective; empirical findings entering with Haack's distinct anchored status — the clues; a third warrant dimension, independent security, guarding against co-validation circularity; a genuineness-of-inquiry channel; capacity nodes beneath finding nodes; the as-tested and scope-adjusted claims as two nodes joined by a warranted inference rather than one node at two confidences; epistemic VOI weighted toward support-factor studies; and — the firmest correction — coherence treated as a diagnostic and never as an objective function, because the world is dappled.

Pearl's critique did not break the architecture, but it disciplined its description. Three of his points must be conceded permanently and written into the essay. The "BN" must be named precisely as the *causal* hierarchy, rungs one through three, and credited with everything it can do, including transportability — the essay's contrast is not EN-versus-BN but EN-*on*-BN, the upper layer modelling the warrant of the lower layer's assumptions. The claim that the BN "cannot represent a confound" must be retired and replaced with the accurate claim: the BN represents *named, modelled* confounds and the *identifiability* question superbly, and what it cannot represent is the warrant of the causal assumptions on which every identifiability result is conditional — which is a different and real gap. And the coherence instrument must be visibly fenced: a diagnostic, never a maximand, on pain of building the conservatism engine Pearl rightly fears.

What survives all of this — what the three readers sharpened and the critic could not dislodge — is the core. A causal Bayesian network, however high up Pearl's ladder, answers questions *conditional on its causal assumptions*. The warrant of those assumptions, the weight as against the balance of the evidence for them, the independent security of that evidence, the genuineness of the inquiry that produced it, the distance the claims have travelled from the nomological machines that made them, and the coherence — diagnostic, not enforced — of the whole: these are the field's epistemic state, they are what a survey of a field must report, and they live on a layer the do-calculus was never built to occupy and never claimed to. Pearl's last word is the one to keep, because it is true and it is ours: an ounce of correct causal assumption outweighs a ton of data. Precisely. The Epistemic Network is the instrument for weighing the ounce.
