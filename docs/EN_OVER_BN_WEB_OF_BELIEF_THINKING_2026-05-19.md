# What an Epistemic Network Reveals That a Bayesian Network Cannot — and That RAG-plus-AI Cannot Either

*David Kirsh, thinking document, 2026-05-19. Prepared in response to the question of what is gained by placing an Epistemic Network (EN) on top of a Bayesian Network (BN): what such a layer can tell us about a scientific field that neither a BN alone nor a retrieval-augmented language model alone can tell us. Candidate input to the master document's epistemics section.*

---

## 1. The question, stated precisely

The Knowledge Atlas maintains, for its corpus, two layers that are easy to conflate and important to separate. The lower layer is Bayesian: a network of variables — environmental conditions, outcome measures, mechanisms — over which one can propagate probabilities and, in the causal case, simulate interventions. The upper layer is epistemic: a network whose nodes are *beliefs* — findings, theories, mechanism claims — and whose edges are relations of *support, contradiction, entailment, and explanatory coherence*, and whose node-values are not probabilities of events but *warranted credences*: degrees of belief that carry, attached to them, the structure of their own justification.

The question is what the second layer buys. It is a fair question, because a Bayesian network is a powerful and well-understood instrument, and because a retrieval-augmented language model can already produce a fluent survey of almost any literature on demand. If the EN is only a prettier BN, or only a structured cache of what an LLM would say anyway, it is not worth its maintenance cost. The claim of this document is that it is neither — that there is a definite and characterizable class of things one can know about a field only by representing the field as a web of belief, and that this class includes, but is not exhausted by, the three cases the question raised: the re-understanding of Value of Information, the way framework-level priors add to or subtract from confidence in a finding, and the way a suspected confound or a biased sample bears on warranted belief.

A note on register before beginning. Most of the *distinctions* drawn below — between the balance and the weight of evidence, between rebutting and undercutting defeat, between a claim as-tested and a claim travelled — are well established in their home literatures in philosophy of science and formal epistemology, and I cite them as such. The *synthesis* — the claim that an EN-over-BN architecture for a live research corpus delivers all of them at once and makes them computable — is the project's design wager, not an established result, and I mark it as a wager wherever it appears. The reader should keep the two apart.

## 2. Three things that are not the same

It clears the ground to say plainly what the three instruments are.

A **Bayesian network** is a representation of a joint probability distribution over a fixed set of variables, factored along a directed acyclic graph (Pearl, 1988). Its semantics is first-order: each node carries a probability of a *state of the world*, and the machinery answers questions of the form "given what I have observed, what should I expect of what I have not?" In its causal interpretation (Pearl, 2009; Spirtes, Glymour, & Scheines, 2000) it also answers "if I were to intervene and set X, what would happen to Y?" A BN is, in the end, an instrument for *uncertainty about the world*.

A **retrieval-augmented language model** — RAG plus an LLM — is an instrument for *fluent synthesis of what has been said*. Given a query it retrieves passages judged similar to the query and composes them into prose. It is fast, broad, and genuinely useful, and it is the natural baseline against which any structured alternative must justify itself. But it has no persistent state between queries, no model of warrant, and no representation of the field other than the corpus of text itself. It mirrors the literature, and it mirrors the literature's emphases — which is to say it mirrors the literature's biases.

An **Epistemic Network**, in the sense the Atlas intends, is an instrument for the *warranted epistemic state of a field*. Its nodes are beliefs; its node-values are credences that decompose into their grounds; its edges are justificatory relations; and it carries, as first-class objects, the things that make a credence the credence it is — the entrenchment of the theories that lend it priors, the defeaters that discount it, the coherence or tension of the surrounding web. It is, in Quine and Ullian's (1978) phrase, a *web of belief*: a structure in which no belief stands alone, in which revision anywhere reverberates everywhere, and in which — this is the part that matters most — nothing is exogenous. The framework, the auxiliary assumptions, even the standards of evidence are themselves in the web and themselves revisable (Quine, 1951).

The three are not competitors arranged on a single scale of quality. They answer different questions. The BN answers *what is true of the world*. The RAG-plus-AI answers *what has been said about the world*. The EN answers *how well, and on what grounds, the field knows what it takes itself to know — and where that knowing is fragile*. Only the last of these is a question about the field as an epistemic object, and it is the question this document is about.

## 3. The core asymmetry: probability of an event versus warrant of a belief

The deepest single difference is this. A Bayesian network node carries one number, and that number does the work of two distinct things that ought to be kept apart.

Consider two findings, each of which the system represents at credence 0.5. The first is a well-replicated effect that genuinely occurs in about half of the relevant cases — a sharp, stable, heavily evidenced 0.5. The second is a contested effect studied twice, once significant and once null, in small samples, with no mechanism and no theory — a 0.5 that expresses near-total ignorance. In a Bayesian network these are the same node value. The machinery cannot tell them apart, because the machinery has one slot per node and the slot is already full.

The distinction the BN cannot draw is old and well understood. Keynes (1921) called it the difference between the *balance* of evidence — which way the evidence points, and how far — and the *weight* of evidence — how much evidence there is, how much of the relevant considerations have been brought to bear. Two credences can have the same balance and utterly different weight. Skyrms (1977, 1980) reconstructed the same idea as *resilience*: a probability is resilient to the degree that it would survive conditioning on further evidence, and a resilient 0.5 and a fragile 0.5 are different epistemic objects even though they are the same number. Joyce (2005) made the point again in arguing that a single probability cannot, on its own, "reflect the evidence" — that the evidential situation has a dimensionality the point-valued credence flattens.

A Bayesian network flattens it by construction. An Epistemic Network does not, because the EN's node-value is not a bare number but a number *with its warrant attached* — and the warrant carries the weight. In the Atlas's V2 schema this is concrete rather than metaphorical: a credence decomposes into mechanism warrant, entrenchment warrant, meta-analytic warrant, and confounding warrant, with a defeat status and a count of supporting and contradicting items in the warrant packet. A 0.5 that is the sum of strong, agreeing, undefeated warrants is a different database row from a 0.5 that is thin and contested, and the system can sort, filter, and survey on the difference. This is the precondition for everything that follows. Once you can represent *how much a credence is worth*, and not only *what the credence is*, you can ask questions about a field that were previously unaskable.

## 4. The finding is not transparent: what a *d* and a *p* cannot carry

The question raised the sharp case directly: a published finding arrives with an effect size *d* and a *p*-value; a confound is suspected, or the sample is known to be unrepresentative; can a Bayesian network make the corresponding *subtraction* from confidence? The answer is instructive, because it is *almost* yes and the gap between "almost" and "yes" is the whole point.

A *d* and a *p* are real evidence, and a Bayesian treatment uses them properly: they parametrize the likelihood — how probable a result like this one would be under the hypothesis and under its negation (Hacking, 1965; Howson & Urbach, 2006). Fed a *d* and a *p*, a BN will move a posterior, and move it by about the right amount *given the study's own model of itself*. That is what *d* and *p* are for.

But notice precisely what *d* and *p* are quantities *of*. They are statistical-conclusion-validity quantities (Shadish, Cook, & Campbell, 2002): they speak to whether, *granting the study's design*, the result is unlikely to be noise. They are silent — completely silent — about internal validity, construct validity, and external validity. A confounded study can have an immaculate *d* and a *p* below .001; the confound does not perturb either number, because the confound is not in the study's model. A WEIRD-sample study (Henrich, Heine, & Norenzayan, 2010) has a perfectly well-behaved *d* and *p*; the unrepresentativeness leaves no trace in them. The intuitive "subtraction" the question asks about — discount this finding because a confound is plausible, because the sample is narrow — cannot be made *from d and p*, because *d* and *p* are not the channel through which that information travels. They never were.

Could a BN make the subtraction by some other route? Here the answer divides, and the division is the heart of the matter. If the confound is *known and named* — if you can write down the confounding variable — then yes: you add it to the graph as a node, and the BN will explain away the effect through it. This is exactly what a causal BN is good at. But the situation that actually obtains, in the great majority of real studies, is not that. It is the *generic suspicion* of an *unmodelled* confound — the standing possibility, which internal-validity analysis exists to interrogate, that something not in anyone's graph is doing the work. That suspicion is not a variable. It is a statement *about the graph itself* — a statement that the graph may be the wrong graph. A Bayesian network cannot represent a doubt about its own structure as a node, because its structure is its frame and the frame is presupposed by every node in it. Philosophers of defeasible reasoning have a precise name for what is going on. Pollock (1987, 1995) distinguished *rebutting* defeaters, which are evidence that a conclusion is false, from *undercutting* defeaters, which are evidence that the *inference* to the conclusion is broken — without bearing, one way or the other, on whether the conclusion happens to be true. A confound is the paradigm undercutting defeater: it does not say "no effect," it says "your route to believing in the effect does not go through." A Bayesian network handles rebutting evidence natively — you condition on it. It has no native representation of undercutting defeat at all, because undercutting defeat is directed at the *warrant link*, and the BN has nodes and edges but no separate object that is "the warrant of this belief" available to be attacked.

The Epistemic Network has exactly that object. The confounding-warrant component and the defeat status in the Atlas schema are a representation of undercutting defeat as a first-class, inspectable, revisable thing. The *d* and the *p* set the as-tested credence; the confounding warrant and the scope metadata apply, through a *different channel*, the discount the *d* and the *p* cannot carry. The slogan is worth stating baldly: **a Bayesian network can update on evidence; an Epistemic Network can additionally update on meta-evidence about whether the evidence is evidence.** That second capacity is not a refinement of the first. It is a different operation, and it is the one that a confound, a biased sample, a demand-characteristics worry, a single-operationalization worry, and a researcher-degrees-of-freedom worry all require.

## 5. Priors that descend from a framework

The question's second case is subtler and, I think, the most revealing of the three. A theoretical framework — the example given was predictive processing — supplies a finding with a prior: under the framework, an effect of this kind is antecedently plausible, or antecedently surprising, and our confidence is correspondingly raised or lowered. Can a Bayesian network represent that?

Superficially, easily: a framework-supplied prior is just a prior, and a BN has priors. Put the number on the hypothesis node and be done. But this superficial answer hides three failures, and the three failures are what motivate the upper layer.

The first failure is that in a Bayesian network the prior is *exogenous and frozen*. It is a number entered from outside the model; it has no provenance inside the model and no life after entry. The Epistemic Network, by contrast, does not store the prior as a number. It stores it as a *live edge from a live node* — an edge of support running from the framework, which is itself a belief in the web with its own credence, its own entrenchment, its own coherence with everything around it. The practical consequence is the one that matters: when the framework's own standing changes — when predictive processing accrues an anomaly, or loses a key piece of support, or is challenged by a rival — the credence of every finding to which it lent a prior *re-derives*. The web propagates. In the BN the prior was a frozen 0.7 and stays 0.7 though the world has moved on; in the EN the prior was an edge, and the edge now carries less. This is just Quine's (1951) holism made operational: the framework is not a vantage point outside the web from which priors are handed down, it is a node *inside* the web, and its tremors are felt at the periphery.

The second failure is that a Bayesian network, handed a framework-prior and a finding-likelihood, will treat them as two independent sources of information and multiply them. Often they are not independent. The finding may have been designed under the framework, analyzed with the framework's preferred measures, and interpreted in the framework's vocabulary — the theory-ladenness of observation that Hanson and Kuhn pressed, and that the modern literature on *use-novelty* has made precise (Worrall, 1985; Mayo, 1996). A finding that was constructed under a framework cannot fully *confirm* that framework, and a framework cannot fully *vouch* for a finding it shaped, because the apparent agreement is partly an artifact of common parentage. A BN that multiplies the prior and the likelihood double-counts the shared ancestry. The EN can represent the dependence — it can record that finding and framework are not independent, that the agreement is partly inherited — and decline the double-count. This requires representing the *provenance* of a finding: was the prediction registered in advance, was the analysis framework-driven, was the result use-novel or merely accommodated. Those are epistemic-network facts. They are not in any *d* and they are not in any *p*.

The third failure is the one the question itself half-states. The questioner asks whether a BN can make the framework-driven *subtraction* given that the finding has a *d* and a *p*. The honest answer is that a BN can install a low prior and so produce a lower posterior — but it cannot represent the subtraction *as a subtraction*, cannot show its working, cannot say *the framework is what cost this finding fifteen points of credence and here is the framework and here is how entrenched it is*. The number comes out lower; the reason does not come out at all. The Epistemic Network keeps the reason, because in the EN the framework-to-finding relation is a first-class edge and the credence is an audit trail rather than a scalar. And keeping the reason is not bookkeeping fastidiousness. It is what lets a student, or a reviewer, or a future version of the corpus *argue with* the subtraction — agree that the confound matters but think fifteen points too steep, or accept the framework's relevance but doubt the framework. A posterior you cannot argue with is not a good instrument for a science that proceeds by argument.

## 6. Value of Information, re-understood

The question put VOI first, and rightly, because VOI is where the difference between the two layers becomes a difference in what it is even *possible to ask*.

The classical, Bayesian Value of Information is a decision-theoretic quantity (Raiffa & Schlaifer, 1961; and, in its information-theoretic form, Lindley, 1956). It is defined relative to a decision and a target variable: the value of an observation is the expected improvement in the decision — or, in Lindley's version, the expected reduction in the entropy of the target — that the observation would bring. It is *predictive* VOI. It answers: which measurement would most sharpen my forecast of the variable I must act on? It is an excellent question and the BN answers it well.

The Epistemic Network permits a different question, and the question raised it exactly: which study would most reduce uncertainty *about something we care about*, where the uncertainty in question is uncertainty *in our warranted belief* and not uncertainty in a prediction. Call it *epistemic* VOI. It is not a refinement of predictive VOI; it is a different functional, computed over a different object, and it comes apart from predictive VOI in at least four ways that a field-survey should care about.

First, its *target* is a belief's warrant, not a prediction's variance. Epistemic VOI asks: which new study would most raise the *weight* — in Keynes's sense of §3 — of a credence we hold, or, just as importantly, which study could most *lower* it by activating a defeater we currently only suspect. A study that converts a low-warrant 0.7 into a high-warrant 0.7 has changed no prediction at all — the point estimate did not move — and yet has done real epistemic work, because the field now *holds* the 0.7 in a way it could not before. Predictive VOI scores that study at nearly zero. Epistemic VOI scores it high. The Atlas's purpose — surveying a field, and equipping a student to choose what to study — is served by the second number, not the first.

Second, epistemic VOI can take *coherence itself* as the target. The EN carries a global coherence score and a count of live tensions; the recent corpus build saw coherence fall, deliberately and correctly, as the network came to *detect* genuine contradictions it had previously been too coarse to see. Against that representation one can define the value of a study as the expected reduction in network tension — the expected number of contradictions it would resolve, weighted by how central the contradicting beliefs are. A study that adjudicates a standing conflict between two findings, or between a finding and a theory, has high epistemic VOI on this definition even if it barely stirs any predictive distribution. There is no way to even *pose* this in a Bayesian network, because a Bayesian network cannot be incoherent — the probability axioms see to that — and so it has no quantity "tension" for a study to be valued against. (More on this in §7.)

Third, epistemic VOI is *entrenchment-sensitive*. Because the EN records which theories are central — which beliefs are expensive to revise — it can recognize that a study which discriminates between two entrenched frameworks has *field-wide* value, since relocating a central node ripples outward through every periphery it supports. A predictive VOI computation sees a study as informing a few local variables and prices it locally. An epistemic VOI computation sees the same study as potentially moving a load-bearing wall and prices the whole building. For a corpus whose job is to survey a field and direct attention, this is the difference between recommending a hundred small confirmatory studies and recommending the one crucial experiment.

Fourth, and underlying the other three, epistemic VOI is *consequentialist about understanding* where classical VOI is *consequentialist about action*. Levi (1980) argued that inquiry has its own utility — informational value — distinct from the practical utilities that decision theory usually serves, and that a rational inquirer trades expected informational value against the risk of error. Epistemic VOI is VOI computed against informational value: the goal is a well-warranted, coherent, mechanism-complete picture, and a study is worth running to the degree it is expected to move us toward that picture. Decision-VOI and understanding-VOI genuinely diverge. A study can sharpen a prediction we will act on while teaching us nothing about why the effect occurs — high decision-VOI, low understanding-VOI. A study can resolve a deep mechanistic tension while changing no decision anyone faces — the reverse. The Atlas, and a COGS 160 student deciding what is worth ten weeks of their life, want understanding-VOI, and understanding-VOI is only definable once the field is represented as a web of warranted belief.

One worked contrast, in the Atlas's own domain. Predictive VOI, asked about the open-plan-office literature, will recommend measuring ambient sound level more precisely, because sound level is the modelled predictor of the performance decrement and a better measurement tightens the forecast. Epistemic VOI, asked the same question, recommends instead the study that holds sound level fixed and varies *speech intelligibility* — because the standing tension in that corner of the web is precisely whether the decrement is driven by loudness or by intelligibility, and the intelligibility study is the one that resolves a construct-level contradiction, relocates belief, and (not incidentally) exposes the shallow-measurement gap that the post-occupancy review independently identified. The two VOI computations do not rank the same study first. The field looks different depending on which question you are entitled to ask, and only the upper layer entitles you to the second.

## 7. Distinctions the question did not name

The three cases the question raised are not the whole of it. Representing a field as a web of belief makes a further set of things visible, and several of them are at least as consequential.

**Entrenchment, and the cost of revision.** A Bayesian network has no notion of entrenchment: every node is updated by the same rule and giving one up costs no more than giving up another. A web of belief is not like that. Quine's (1951) holism, and Lakatos's (1978) reconstruction of it as a *hard core* protected by a *belt* of auxiliary hypotheses, both say that beliefs differ in how costly they are to abandon, and that rational revision is conservative — it spends the periphery to spare the centre. The EN records entrenchment explicitly (in the recent build, Attention Restoration Theory near the ceiling, predictive processing close behind). This lets the EN say something the BN cannot: not merely that a new finding is *surprising* — low likelihood — but that it is *expensive*, and that the field consequently faces a genuine choice between doubting the finding and paying to revise the theory. Sometimes doubting the finding is right (cold fusion); sometimes it is the costly revision that is overdue (continental drift). The EN does not settle which; it makes the choice *visible as a choice*, flags the finding as pressing on a load-bearing wall, and lets the survey locate every such pressure point. A BN simply lowers a posterior somewhere and reports nothing about what just happened structurally.

**Coherence as a global property: a field has a shape.** A Bayesian network, once conditioned, is coherent of necessity — the axioms guarantee it — and so it cannot represent a field as containing a real, unresolved contradiction. Feed a BN two conflicting results and it does not register a conflict; it returns a posterior. The Epistemic Network can hold a contradiction *as* a contradiction — two beliefs each with standing warrant, and incompatible — and can score the whole network's tension. Thagard's (1989, 2000) theory of explanatory coherence is the worked-out account of how this can be made computational: coherence as a global constraint-satisfaction property over a network of explanatory and contradictory relations. With that property in hand, a survey can do something genuinely new — it can report the *epistemic weather* of a field. A high-tension, low-coherence field is a field in or near what Kuhn (1962) called crisis: anomalies accumulating, the central theories straining. A high-coherence field is settled, perhaps complacently so. The Atlas's coherence score *falling* during a build — 0.54 to 0.42 — was not a regression; it was the instrument acquiring the resolution to see tension that was there all along. No Bayesian posterior, and no language-model synthesis, ever reports rising incoherence, because neither has the concept.

**Scope, and the travelling of claims.** Nancy Cartwright (1999; Cartwright & Hardie, 2012) insisted that a result is established, in the first instance, only for the narrow setup that produced it — under the particular "nomological machine" of the experiment — and that its credence for any wider claim is a separate and usually smaller thing, because claims *travel* badly out of the contexts that warrant them. The Atlas builds this in as the three-credence decomposition: a finding's *as-tested* credence, its *scope-adjusted* credence once the sample and setting are taken into account, and its *network* credence once one asks whether it composes with the other mechanisms the field believes. The questioner's population-bias case is exactly the as-tested-to-scope-adjusted gap. A Bayesian network has one number per node and so cannot represent the *same claim at different scopes* — a variable is a variable; it does not come in a lab edition and a world edition. The EN tracks the claim's travel, and a field-survey built on it can report how far, on average, this field's claims have been shown to travel — which, for a field that aspires to inform building design from laboratory VR studies, is close to the most important thing there is to know about it.

**The shape of ignorance.** A Bayesian network represents the variables it has. A retrieval model represents the documents that exist. Neither can represent, as a positive object, *what the field has not asked* — and yet the structure of a field's inattention is one of the most useful things a survey can deliver. Because the EN sits over an explicit construct-and-measure ontology as well as a warrant graph, it can see the *structural holes*: the IV-by-DV intersections with no studies, the mechanism links asserted in theory but never directly tested, the constructs measured always by one shallow proxy and never by the deeper instrument the literature already possesses. The post-occupancy review's central inventory — the field measures sound level but not speech intelligibility, measures illuminance but not the melanopic dose, measures presence-of-a-window but not the content of the view — is precisely an epistemic-network observation. It is a claim about the silhouette of the field's attention, and it is derivable only from a representation that holds together what *has* been studied and what *could* be. The eight POE-EXT extraction items now seeded into the substitution graph are, in this light, holes the EN was able to see and the other two instruments were not.

**Explainable credence: the warrant packet as an audit trail.** A Bayesian posterior is, in principle, traceable through the conditional probability tables, but what it traces to is a story about the world. An EN credence traces to a story about *the grounds* — one can ask "why 0.6?" and receive "0.85 as-tested, less 0.15 for an activated population-scope defeater, less 0.10 for absent mechanism warrant, plus 0.05 for meta-analytic support, adjusted for coherence with an entrenched framework." This is explainable epistemics, and for a teaching instrument it is not a luxury but the entire pedagogical point: a COGS 160 student should see not that "the field believes X at 0.6" but the *anatomy* of the 0.6, because the anatomy is the part you can learn from and the part you can contest.

**Counterfactual epistemics.** A causal Bayesian network supports intervention — the do-operator — on world variables: what happens to Y if I set X. An Epistemic Network supports the analogous operation one level up: intervention on the *belief* network. Because the EN models warrant structure, one can ask counterfactuals over the epistemic state — what finding, at what warrant, would flip belief B; which entrenched theory, abandoned, would most reduce total tension; if the suspected confound in study S were confirmed, how far through the web does the damage propagate. This is something like a do-calculus for justification rather than for causation, and epistemic VOI (§6) is simply its expectation form: VOI asks the counterfactual "were this study run, how would the web move?" and averages over the study's possible results. A BN does counterfactuals on the world; the EN does them on the field's knowledge of the world.

**Typing the disagreement.** When two findings conflict, a Bayesian network absorbs both into a posterior and the conflict vanishes into a number. The EN can instead *diagnose* the conflict: is it a power problem — one study underpowered; a scope problem — different populations or settings, so both true at their own scope; a construct problem — the two studies used one word for two different things, the *jingle* fallacy, or two words for one thing, the *jangle* fallacy (Thorndike, 1904; Kelley, 1927); a genuine moderator — the effect real but conditional; or a true anomaly. The Atlas's construct nodes, scope metadata, and jingle-jangle proliferation warnings are what make the diagnosis possible. And the diagnosis is itself a survey-level finding: to be able to say "the disagreements in this corner of the field are overwhelmingly construct mismatch, not genuine empirical conflict" is to say something both damning and immediately useful, and only the EN can say it.

**Severity, and why it lives on the upper layer.** Mayo (1996, 2018) argues that what licenses a claim is not the bare probability of the data but the *severity* of the test the claim has passed — the degree to which the procedure would, with high probability, have produced a result discrediting the claim had the claim been false. Severity is not a property of an outcome; it is a property of a *test in relation to an error*, and two studies with the very same *d* and *p* can have sharply different severity depending on design, blinding, pre-registration, and the space of errors left uninterrogated. The VOI panel's convergence on "severity at the centre" was a recognition of exactly this. Severity cannot live on the Bayesian layer, because the Bayesian layer sees the outcome and its likelihood, not the test's error-catching capacity. It is an upper-layer, warrant-level quantity, and an EN is the natural place to carry it.

## 8. Surveying a field as a web of belief

Put the pieces together and the yield is a set of *field-level diagnostics* that have no counterpart in either a Bayesian network — which models a phenomenon, not a field's knowledge of it — or a retrieval model — which mirrors a discourse, not a structure of warrant.

One can compute, and the Atlas is built to compute, a **tension map**: where in the field the live contradictions are concentrated. A **warrant distribution**: whether the field's belief is mostly high-weight or whether it is, on inspection, a great expanse of fragile 0.5s. An **entrenchment-versus-evidence audit**: whether the theories the field treats as central are the ones the evidence actually supports, or whether there is a load-bearing wall built of cardboard — a theory everyone cites and few have tested. A **defeater census**: how much of the field's belief is, right now, under live undercutting defeat — suspected confounds, awaiting replication, demonstrated only in WEIRD samples. A **mechanism-completeness measure**: how much of what the field believes is a warranted mechanism and how much is a black-box association awaiting one. A **VOI landscape**: every feasible next study, ranked by expected movement of the web. And a standing check for what Meehl (1990) called the *crud factor* — the condition, common in soft psychology, in which everything correlates with everything, so that the bare Bayesian associations are nearly empty of content and only the warranted, mechanism-bearing structure carries information.

Every one of these is a sentence about the field as an epistemic object. None of them is a sentence a Bayesian network can form, because a Bayesian network has no term that denotes the field's epistemic state. And none of them is a sentence a retrieval model can reliably form either — which is the last contrast, and it deserves its own section.

## 9. Why retrieval-augmented AI cannot stand in for the Epistemic Network

It is tempting to think a sufficiently good RAG-plus-LLM makes the EN redundant: ask it for a critical survey and it will, fluently, give you one. The temptation should be resisted, for reasons that are structural rather than matters of current model quality.

A retrieval model has **no persistent epistemic state**. It re-derives a query-local synthesis each time and discards it. It therefore cannot carry a coherence score, a tension count, an entrenchment ranking, or a warrant distribution, because all of those are properties of a *maintained* structure and the retrieval model maintains none. It can describe; it cannot keep books.

A retrieval model is **weighted by what is said, not by what is warranted**. Its native currency is recurrence and similarity: the claims it surfaces and the confidence with which it surfaces them track how often, how prominently, and how fluently things have been written. It therefore inherits, faithfully, the literature's pathologies — the file-drawer problem, the citation cascade behind a single underpowered original, the megaphone of the well-funded theory. The EN, with explicit warrant decomposition, is built precisely to *correct* for this: it can display a finding that is widely cited and poorly warranted, or one barely cited and well warranted, because it scores warrant on a channel independent of citation. A retrieval model has only the one channel, and so it cannot, even in principle, separate *consensus* from *warrant* — and the gap between a field's consensus and a field's warrant is one of the most important things a survey can report, because a low-warrant consensus is the most dangerous epistemic object a field can contain.

A retrieval model **cannot compute Value of Information** in the sense that matters, because VOI requires a model of what is currently believed and how firmly, plus the counterfactual machinery to ask how a possible result would move that model. Retrieval recovers text; it does not maintain a belief state against which expected information could be scored.

And a retrieval model **does not propagate**. When a new study lands, the retrieval index simply has one more document. In the EN the new study updates warrants, may activate or discharge a defeater, may shift a theory's entrenchment, may raise or lower coherence — and those effects propagate to beliefs the new study never mentioned. The EN has a memory of the *justificatory structure*; the retrieval model has a memory of *text*. The first can be revised in the disciplined, traceable, holistic way that Quine described and that scientific inference actually requires. The second can only be re-queried.

The fair summary is that a retrieval-augmented model is an excellent *mirror* of a field and the Epistemic Network is a *model* of it — and the difference between a mirror and a model is that you can run a model forward, intervene on it, and ask it what it does not yet know.

---

## 10. A caution: what the upper layer does not replace, and what it risks

Intellectual honesty requires three qualifications, because the argument above could be read as triumphalism and should not be.

First, the Epistemic Network does not *replace* the Bayesian layer; it *sits on* it and depends on it. The object-level causal and predictive work — estimating an effect, modelling a mechanism, simulating an intervention on the world — is the BN's proper job and the EN does not do it. The claim is that the BN is necessary and not sufficient for surveying a field, not that it is dispensable. A great deal of what a working scientist needs is exactly first-order uncertainty about the world, and for that the BN, not the EN, is the instrument.

Second, the EN's own quantities are themselves estimates, and they should be worn lightly. A warrant decomposition is a model of justification, not a measurement of it; a coherence score is a modelling choice with parameters; an entrenchment ranking is an inference from citation and revision history that could be drawn otherwise. Where the credences in the web descend from a *simulated* expert panel rather than a real one — as several of the Atlas's do at present — they are explicitly provisional, and the system is right to label them so. The EN's advantage is not that its numbers are true where the BN's are false; it is that the EN represents the *kind* of thing — warrant, weight, defeat, coherence, scope, entrenchment — that the BN cannot represent at all. A representation can be the right kind of representation and still be imperfectly calibrated, and the EN currently is.

Third, the distinctions this document leans on are of two epistemic statuses and the reader should not let the prose blur them. That the balance of evidence differs from its weight (Keynes; Skyrms; Joyce); that undercutting defeat differs from rebutting defeat (Pollock); that claims travel badly out of their warranting context (Cartwright); that explanatory coherence is a global constraint-satisfaction property (Thagard); that severity is a property of tests rather than of outcomes (Mayo) — these are well-established positions in their home literatures, and they are cited as such. That an EN-over-BN architecture for a live research corpus delivers all of them at once, computably, and that doing so genuinely improves how a field can be surveyed and taught — that is the Knowledge Atlas's design wager. It is a wager I think is sound, and the post-occupancy gap inventory and the substitution graph are early evidence that it pays. But it is a wager, it is being tested by building the thing, and it should be described to the COGS 160 students, and in the master document, as exactly that: a bet on a way of representing what a field knows, not a discovered fact about it.

---

## 11. Summary

A Bayesian network answers what is true of the world; a retrieval-augmented language model answers what has been said about the world; an Epistemic Network answers how well, and on what grounds, a field knows what it takes itself to know. The three are not rivals on one scale. The upper layer earns its keep by representing a definite class of things the lower layer and the language model cannot: the *weight* of a credence as against its *balance*; *undercutting* defeat — the confound, the biased sample — as against mere counter-evidence; priors that are *live edges from a framework* rather than frozen exogenous numbers, and that re-derive when the framework moves; *epistemic* Value of Information, computed against warrant and coherence rather than against predictive variance, and so capable of asking which study would most reduce uncertainty in what the field *believes*; the *entrenchment* of theories and hence the *cost* of revision; *coherence* and *tension* as global properties, so that a field can be diagnosed as settled or in crisis; the *scope* at which a claim has actually been warranted, and how far it has travelled; the *shape of the field's ignorance*, the structural holes; the credence as an *audit trail* one can argue with; *counterfactual epistemics*, intervention on the belief network itself; the *type* of a disagreement; and the *severity* of the tests a claim has survived. Each is unavailable below. Together they are what it means to survey a field as a web of belief rather than as a probability distribution or as a corpus of text — and surveying it that way is the thing the Knowledge Atlas exists to do.
