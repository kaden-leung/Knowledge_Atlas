# Post-Occupancy Evaluation: A Review of the Best Academic Work, with a Knowledge-Atlas-Grounded Inventory of the Things POE Could Measure but Usually Does Not

*David Kirsh — draft prepared 2026-05-19. Comments welcome.*

---

## Abstract

Post-Occupancy Evaluation (POE) — also called Building Performance Evaluation in its mature variants — is the empirical investigation, after handover, of whether a building actually does what it was supposed to do for the people who inhabit it. The field has a fifty-year arc: from Zimring and Reizenstein's (1980) early framework, through Preiser's (1988) systematisation, the United Kingdom's PROBE programme of the late 1990s (Bordass, Cohen, Standeven, & Leaman, 2001a, 2001b), the Center for the Built Environment's (CBE) web-based occupant survey at Berkeley (Zagreus, Huizenga, Arens, & Lehrer, 2004), Vischer's (2007, 2008) functional-comfort reframing, the Soft Landings programme (BSRIA, 2018), and the recent confrontation with the "performance gap" in supposedly green buildings (Newsham, Mancini, & Birt, 2009; Frankel & Turner, 2008). The first part of this document is a review of that arc. The second part — the part that will most interest the Knowledge Atlas project — is an inventory of cases drawn from our own corpus where the standard POE instrument measures a *surface proxy* for a phenomenon when the underlying research literature has long since articulated a deeper construct that better predicts what occupants actually experience. The classical example is noise: POE asks "Is it too noisy?" when the variable that actually predicts task decrement is the *speech transmission index* of irrelevant speech (Hongisto, 2005; Haka et al., 2009). A presentation built around this inventory should make the same move sensorily and methodologically across every IEQ axis. The third part catalogues human-centred methods — physiological, psychometric, implicit, behavioural — that operationalise the deeper constructs, including some (Implicit Association Tests, Q-sort and 20-sort tasks, adaptive-preference elicitation in the Sen sense) that scarcely appear in the POE literature at all.

## Part I — A Review of the Best Academic Work on Post-Occupancy Evaluation

### Origins: from accident-investigation to systematic feedback

POE began, like much of environmental psychology, in the rubble of an unsatisfactory building. Pruitt-Igoe in St. Louis, Britain's high-rise housing estates of the 1960s, and the post-Vietnam American mental hospitals all furnished cases in which the building, however lawful and well-engineered, made the lives of its inhabitants worse. Zimring and Reizenstein's (1980) review in *Environment and Behavior* — still one of the most-cited early statements of the field — defined POE as the systematic gathering of information about completed and occupied buildings, distinguished it from architectural criticism (which is evaluative but unsystematic) and from human-factors research (which is systematic but rarely focused on whole settings), and proposed three levels of investigation that have survived essentially intact: indicative, investigative, and diagnostic. The indicative level is a brief site visit and walkthrough; the investigative is a structured occupant survey with comparative reference data; the diagnostic is a multi-method, multi-investigator study aiming at causal claims. (Google Scholar reports roughly 600 citations for Zimring and Reizenstein, 1980, depending on the database snapshot.)

Wolfgang Preiser's *Post-Occupancy Evaluation* (1988) — with Rabinowitz and White — codified Zimring and Reizenstein's three-tier scheme into a practitioner manual and is the work most often cited as the field's foundational textbook (Preiser, Rabinowitz, & White, 1988; ≈1,500 citations). Preiser's later collaboration with Vischer in *Assessing Building Performance* (Preiser & Vischer, 2005; ≈1,200 citations) repositioned POE within a six-phase Building Performance Evaluation (BPE) framework spanning strategic planning, programme review, design review, post-construction review, post-occupancy evaluation proper, and facilities-management review. The reframing matters: it explicitly denies that POE is something one does *to* a building after the architects have left, and instead embeds evaluation in every phase, with the post-occupancy phase only the most empirically rich.

### The PROBE studies and BUS methodology

The single body of work that did most to operationalise POE as a comparative-database enterprise was the Post-Occupancy Review of Buildings and their Engineering (PROBE) series, conducted in the United Kingdom between 1995 and 2002 by Bill Bordass, Adrian Leaman, and colleagues, and published primarily in *Building Services Journal* and later in *Building Research & Information* (Bordass, Cohen, Standeven, & Leaman, 2001a, 2001b; Cohen, Standeven, Bordass, & Leaman, 2001). PROBE evaluated some twenty buildings — offices, schools, a mixture of "advanced" and conventional designs — using a fixed methodology: energy and water benchmarking against CIBSE TM22; a structured walkthrough by experts in services engineering, fabric, and use; a fault-tree review of management; and most importantly the Building Use Studies (BUS) Occupant Survey (Leaman & Bordass, 2007). BUS asks occupants to score, on a seven-point semantic-differential scale, their satisfaction with thermal, lighting, acoustic, air-quality, layout, and overall environmental quality, and includes items on perceived health, productivity, and personal control. The BUS dataset now includes several hundred buildings and permits benchmarking against the median building or the upper quartile, which is one of the few defences against the *every-building-is-special* objection that has always dogged POE practice. The "killer variables" paper (Leaman & Bordass, 1999) — which identified personal control over thermal, lighting, and acoustic conditions as the single largest predictor of building satisfaction, eclipsing even the variance attributable to whether the absolute environmental measurements met code — has been one of POE's most consequential findings. Note that this is a finding *consistent across hundreds of buildings*, but the absolute effect size and its policy implications continue to be debated; Newsham, Birt, and Veitch (2012), in a careful CBE-database reanalysis, qualified the personal-control claim by showing that the effect depends materially on workplace type and on whether the controls actually function.

### The CBE Occupant Survey

Across the Atlantic, the Center for the Built Environment at the University of California, Berkeley developed in 1999 what became the dominant North-American POE instrument: the CBE Occupant IEQ Survey (Zagreus, Huizenga, Arens, & Lehrer, 2004; Goins & Moezzi, 2013; Graham, Parkinson, & Schiavon, 2021). It is web-based, takes about ten minutes, covers seven IEQ domains (thermal, air quality, acoustics, lighting, cleanliness, spatial layout, furnishings) plus general satisfaction and self-reported productivity. It branches conditionally — when a respondent is dissatisfied with a domain, the instrument asks why and offers an open-ended response box. The CBE database, with over 90,000 respondents across more than 900 buildings (Graham et al., 2021; ≈300 citations for the twenty-year-retrospective), is the largest standardised IEQ-satisfaction dataset in existence. Its core virtue is benchmarking, and CBE's *Occupant Survey & Building Benchmarking* programme has been used by federal facilities (GSA), university campuses, and corporate real-estate portfolios to identify outliers and to prioritise renovation budgets. It has, however, been criticised on three grounds that bear directly on the present review. First, it is purely a satisfaction instrument: it tells you that occupants are unhappy with the acoustics but not *why*, in any sense that an acoustician could act on. Second, by giving every IEQ domain equal weight in its summary visualisation it implicitly equates a domain in which the field has rich physical-acoustic models (acoustics) with one in which the field's understanding is still primarily psychological (spatial layout). Third, by relying on self-report it captures the layer of awareness that is reportable, and misses the layer of physiological and cognitive response that the occupant cannot introspect on.

### The Vischer reframing: functional comfort and the workspace

Jacqueline Vischer's series of papers and her book *Space Meets Status* (Vischer, 2005, 2007, 2008; the 2008 *Building Research & Information* essay has roughly 800 citations) reframed POE from a satisfaction-and-complaint instrument into a *fit* instrument. The framework distinguishes three levels of comfort: physical comfort (basic environmental adequacy — does the temperature meet code, does the noise level fall below the threshold of damage); functional comfort (does the workspace support the tasks the occupant is trying to perform); and psychological comfort (does the occupant feel a sense of belonging, control, identity, and territoriality). The reframing matters because, in Vischer's argument, every building delivered to ISO and ASHRAE compliance is by construction physically comfortable; what varies, and what POE should therefore measure, is functional and psychological fit. The Vischer reframing also opens the door explicitly to qualitative methods — Q-sorts, walking interviews, observational mapping — that the BUS and CBE traditions had largely excluded in favour of standardised quantitative survey items.

### The Soft Landings turn

The Soft Landings framework, formalised by BSRIA in 2009 and revised in 2018 (BSRIA, 2018; Way & Bordass, 2005), is technically a procurement and handover protocol rather than a POE instrument, but it is the most consequential institutional move toward making POE routine. Soft Landings mandates that the design and construction team remain engaged with the building through extended aftercare — typically three years — and that POE be commissioned at twelve and thirty-six months. Government Soft Landings became a procurement requirement for UK central-government estate from 2016, and the framework has been widely adopted internationally for public-sector work. Its diagnostic premise is one that the present review takes seriously: that the well-publicised *performance gap* between designed and as-operated buildings (Carbon Trust, 2011; van Dronkelaar, Dowson, Spataru, & Mumovic, 2016) is not primarily a problem of design quality but of the abrupt severance of feedback at handover. If the architects never learn how the building performs, they cannot improve.

### The performance-gap literature

The performance-gap literature — though more often framed in energy terms than in human-experience terms — is methodologically important to a serious POE programme because it instituted the practice of comparing predicted to measured performance at the building-by-building level. Newsham, Mancini, and Birt's (2009) reanalysis of the US Green Building Council's dataset of 100 LEED-certified buildings is the single most-cited paper in this area (≈600 citations) and concluded that, although LEED buildings on average used 18–39% less energy than conventional counterparts, between 28% and 35% of LEED buildings used *more* energy than their conventional comparables. The work has had two enduring effects: it disabused the green-building community of the assumption that certification implied performance, and it gave POE a firmer institutional foothold as the only credible mechanism for closing the loop. Frankel and Turner's (2008) parallel work, and Newsham et al.'s (2009) follow-on, have made the energy performance-gap claim about as well-established as findings in this field get, with broad agreement on the magnitude and direction of the gap and continuing debate about the mechanisms (operational behaviour, equipment over-sizing, control complexity, occupancy patterns, climate sensitivity).

### Specific instrumental and methodological strands worth singling out

A few other lines of work deserve mention because they form the methodological tributaries that feed the deeper-measurement inventory in Part II.

Heschong Mahone Group's daylighting-and-student-performance studies (Heschong, Wright, & Okura, 2002; Heschong, 2003) showed, in three school districts and over 21,000 students, that classrooms with the highest amount of daylight had reading and mathematics scores between 7% and 20% higher than the dimmest classrooms, after controlling for fifty potential covariates. These studies, contested when first published, have been substantially replicated (Mott, Robinson, Walden, Burnette, & Rutherford, 2012) and form one of the empirical pillars supporting the move from illuminance-only lighting evaluation to a richer set of measures including daylight autonomy, view content, and circadian effects.

Wargocki and Wyon's series of laboratory studies on ventilation, perceived air quality, and cognitive performance (Wargocki, Wyon, Sundell, Clausen, & Fanger, 2000; Wargocki & Wyon, 2007a, 2007b) produced an effect size — roughly 1.7% improvement in office task performance per doubling of outdoor airflow rate — that has been reproduced in classroom (Bakó-Biró, Clements-Croome, Kochhar, Awbi, & Williams, 2012) and field (Allen et al., 2016) studies and is one of the most policy-actionable POE findings in existence.

Hongisto's (2005) speech-transmission-index model of task performance under irrelevant speech, validated by Haka et al. (2009) in a controlled office mock-up and refined more recently (Hongisto, Keränen, & Haapakangas, 2020), demonstrated that performance on short-term-memory and reading tasks degrades almost linearly with STI between values of about 0.20 and 0.60. This is the canonical example of measuring the right physical variable: the *intelligibility* of background speech rather than its loudness.

De Dear and Brager's (1998, 2002) adaptive thermal-comfort work — the basis of ASHRAE 55's adaptive section — established that occupants of naturally ventilated buildings tolerate a far wider range of temperatures than the Fanger PMV/PPD model predicts, and that thermal comfort depends as much on availability of adaptive opportunities (operable windows, fans, dress code, schedule flexibility) as on the absolute thermal environment.

Wienold and Christoffersen's (2006) Daylight Glare Probability metric and its subsequent validation across many test sites (Wienold et al., 2019) replaced the older Daylight Glare Index with a probabilistic metric that takes vertical eye illuminance and contrast distribution into account and that correlates strongly with occupant reports.

The CIE S 026 standard (CIE, 2018) operationalised the melanopic Equivalent Daylight Illuminance (m-EDI) as the SI-compliant metric of circadian-effective light, drawing on Lucas et al.'s (2014) consensus statement and now appearing in workplace recommendations of m-EDI 250 lux at the eye during the working day (Brown et al., 2022).

Kang and colleagues' (2016; Aletta, Kang, & Axelsson, 2016) work on soundscape — eventually codified in the ISO 12913 series (ISO, 2014, 2018) — reframed acoustic POE from a noise-control to a context-and-meaning enterprise: a quiet space is not in itself a good space, and a sonically rich one may be restorative rather than disturbing.

### Critique: the standard POE survey is shallow by construction

A fair reading of fifty years of POE research must conclude that the field has produced two great practical successes — the BUS/PROBE and CBE survey traditions — and one great methodological limitation. The limitation is that the standardised questionnaire item, however well-validated, captures only what the occupant can report at the moment of asking. It captures the *layer of phenomenal awareness*. The deeper variables that the physical-environment and psychophysiological literatures have shown to drive task decrement, stress response, and long-term health are typically not measured at all in standard POE, partly because doing so requires instrumentation that sits awkwardly with the cost structure of routine post-occupancy work, and partly because the survey tradition has not yet absorbed the methodological repertoire of cognitive neuroscience and environmental psychophysiology. This is the gap that Part II of this document inventories.

## Part II — The Shallow-Measurement Inventory

What follows is a list of cases in which the routine POE measure is a surface proxy for an underlying construct that the research literature — including the body of work indexed in the Knowledge Atlas corpus — has shown to be the real driver of human experience. The corpus, as currently indexed (760 articles across nine environmental dimensions: spatial form, luminous, thermal & air quality, acoustic, nature & biophilia, material & surface, social-spatial, environmental control, and multisensory), is rich in some of these contrasts and silent on others. Where the corpus is silent or thin, I flag this explicitly so we know where extraction is needed. Items are numbered for ease of reference in a later presentation.

**1. Noise level versus speech intelligibility (acoustic).** Standard POE asks whether occupants are bothered by noise and may, in better practice, log ambient A-weighted sound pressure level. The variable that actually predicts cognitive decrement is the *speech transmission index* (STI) of irrelevant background speech: performance on short-term memory and reading tasks falls sharply between STI 0.20 and 0.60 and plateaus thereafter (Hongisto, 2005; Haka et al., 2009). The same room at the same SPL can be cognitively benign or destructive depending on whether the talkers are intelligible. *Corpus presence: 14 evidence rows touch speech intelligibility directly; reverberation 10; soundscape 123.*

**2. Sound volume versus the irrelevant-speech effect (acoustic, cognitive).** A related but distinct shallow-deep pair: the cognitive cost of background speech is largely *not* driven by attentional capture by the content but by phonological-loop interference, which is an automatic, content-blind process (Jones, Madden, & Miles, 1992; Banbury & Berry, 1998). POE that classifies "the office has chatter" as a satisfaction problem misses that chatter at speech-recognisable rate disrupts working memory whether or not anyone notices being distracted.

**3. Acoustic ambient level versus speech privacy (acoustic, social).** Open-plan occupants complain about lack of privacy more than they complain about loudness. The relevant construct, *speech privacy*, is measured by the Articulation Index or its successor metric and depends on the *ratio* of intruding speech to local masking, not on the absolute level of either (Cavanaugh, Farrell, Hirtle, & Watters, 1962, the original AI work; modernised in Bradley & Gover, 2010). Whitespace standards (ANSI S3.5) exist; POE almost never measures against them.

**4. Reverberation time versus reverberation-content interaction (acoustic).** RT60 is itself rarely measured in POE; when it is, it is reported as a single building-average number. The literature on classroom acoustics shows that RT60 interacts with talker-to-listener distance and with student age — children below about ten years require shorter RT60 than adults for equivalent intelligibility (Klatte, Bergström, & Lachmann, 2013; Shield & Dockrell, 2008) — and that uniform-RT spaces are not necessarily desirable when the acoustic programme calls for variable intimacy.

**5. Illuminance versus melanopic equivalent daylight illuminance (luminous).** POE measures horizontal desk illuminance in lux, sometimes vertical eye illuminance. The variable that predicts circadian phase and alertness is melanopic EDI, which depends on the spectral power distribution of the source as weighted by the ipRGC action spectrum (Lucas et al., 2014; CIE S 026, 2018). A LEED-compliant 300-lux desk under a low-CCT LED can deliver only a small fraction of the circadian stimulus of the same illuminance under daylight (Brown et al., 2022). *Corpus presence: 52 evidence rows on circadian; 68 on illuminance; 66 on CCT.*

**6. Lux versus correlated colour temperature and visual cognition (luminous, cognitive).** Equal illuminance at different CCTs produces measurably different alertness, mood, and cognitive-task profiles (Mills, Tomkins, & Schlangen, 2007; Chellappa et al., 2011). POE that records lux but not CCT throws away the dimension along which lighting designers have the most actionable control. *Corpus presence: 66 evidence rows.*

**7. Daylight autonomy versus daylight glare probability (luminous).** Daylight autonomy (the fraction of working hours with daylight above threshold) is a credit-earning metric in green-rating systems. It is silent on the *opposite* failure mode — discomfort glare — and a high-daylight-autonomy room can be a high-glare room. DGP, with thresholds at 35% (imperceptible), 40% (disturbing), and 45% (intolerable), is the appropriate complementary metric (Wienold & Christoffersen, 2006; Wienold et al., 2019). *Corpus presence: 3 evidence rows on glare proper — a corpus gap worth flagging.*

**8. View presence/absence versus view content (luminous, biophilic).** The CBE survey asks whether the occupant has access to a view. The literature has been clear since Ulrich's (1984) hospital-window study that what matters is the *content* of the view — natural versus built, distance versus proximity, vegetated versus paved — and the degree to which the view affords a "soft fascination" that supports attention restoration (Kaplan, 1995; Hartig, Mitchell, de Vries, & Frumkin, 2014). Ulrich's original paper is one of the most-cited in environmental psychology (≈4,000 citations). *Corpus presence: 137 evidence rows on biophilia and view-related content.*

**9. Flicker as a metric versus temporal light modulation (luminous, neural).** LED retrofits have reintroduced flicker as an issue in built environments — most occupants cannot consciously detect it above about 80 Hz, but the visual system processes flicker up to several hundred Hertz, and demonstrable effects on EEG, eye fatigue, and migraine occur in non-naïve populations (Wilkins, Veitch, & Lehman, 2010). IEEE 1789 specifies recommended modulation depth as a function of frequency. POE almost never measures this. *Corpus presence: 8 evidence rows.*

**10. PMV/PPD versus the adaptive opportunity (thermal).** The Fanger Predicted Mean Vote / Predicted Percentage Dissatisfied is the basis of ASHRAE 55's non-adaptive section. De Dear and Brager (1998, 2002) showed that in naturally ventilated buildings, occupants tolerate a much wider thermal range than PMV predicts, and that the predictor of comfort is the *availability* of adaptive opportunity — operable windows, ceiling fans, dress code, schedule control. POE typically measures whether the temperature is within the comfort band; it almost never measures the affordances for adaptation, which the research shows are the actual driver. *Corpus presence: 1 evidence row on adaptive thermal comfort — a corpus gap.*

**11. Operative temperature versus mean radiant temperature and radiant asymmetry (thermal).** Most thermostats and most POE instruments measure air temperature. The body integrates over radiant temperature, especially in spaces with cold-window or warm-floor asymmetries; ASHRAE 55 specifies acceptable radiant-asymmetry limits (10 K head-to-foot for warm ceilings, 5 K for cold ceilings) that are routinely violated in spaces with single-glazed envelopes and that no one ever measures (Halawa, van Hoof, & Soebarto, 2014).

**12. Air temperature versus thermal pleasure / alliesthesia (thermal, affect).** The classical comfort literature treats thermal experience as a single dimension running from "too cold" to "too hot" with a comfort plateau in the middle. Cabanac's alliesthesia work (Cabanac, 1971; recently revisited by Parkinson, de Dear, & Brager, 2020) shows that a thermal stimulus is hedonically pleasurable to the extent that it corrects a thermoregulatory deviation — a warm drink is pleasurable when one is cold, displeasurable when one is hot. The implication for POE is large: a thermally uniform building, designed to never elicit a deviation, may also never elicit thermal pleasure, which is itself a positive contributor to satisfaction.

**13. CO₂ as ventilation proxy versus actual perceived air quality (IAQ).** CO₂ is easy to measure and is treated as a sufficient proxy for adequacy of fresh-air supply. The literature has known since Fanger (1988) that human bio-effluents and material emissions are the relevant pollutants in many indoor settings, and that they are measured in olfs (sources) and decipols (perceived concentration). Wargocki and Wyon's series of studies (Wargocki et al., 2000; Wargocki & Wyon, 2007a, 2007b) demonstrated cognitive performance improvements of roughly 1.7% per doubling of outdoor airflow at typical office concentrations, independent of CO₂ within the ranges tested. *Corpus presence: 3 evidence rows mention CO₂ or ventilation rate; 12 mention olfactory or perceived air quality — surprisingly thin.*

**14. CO₂ versus volatile organic compounds and particulate matter (IAQ).** Allen et al.'s (2016) COGfx studies showed that doubling ventilation halves CO₂ *and* halves VOC concentration, and that cognitive performance scales with VOC reduction at levels well below current OSHA standards. PM2.5 is independently associated with cardiovascular and cognitive endpoints (Power et al., 2018). Standard POE almost never measures VOCs or PM, partly because the measurement instruments have only recently become inexpensive enough. *Corpus presence: 0 evidence rows mention VOC or PM directly — a flat corpus gap.*

**15. Air freshness ratings versus olfactory air quality (IAQ).** Standard POE air-quality items ask occupants to rate "freshness" on a Likert scale. The olfactory literature distinguishes intensity, hedonic tone, and identifiability, and shows that occupants rate buildings as having poor air quality even when measured pollutant levels are well below threshold, when the *odour quality* signals biological or chemical contamination (Bluyssen, 2014). *Corpus presence: 12 evidence rows touch olfactory dimensions.*

**16. Crowding density versus perceived crowding under control (social-spatial).** POE asks about occupant density (square metres per person). The literature since Stokols (1972) and Baum and Davis (1980) has shown that perceived crowding is dissociable from density: density is a physical measure, crowding is a psychological response that depends on perceived control, predictability, and the social meaning of the proximate others. The same density that is restorative in a café is stressful in an open-plan office. *Corpus presence: 51 evidence rows.*

**17. Layout satisfaction versus prospect-refuge (spatial).** Spatial-layout questions on the CBE survey ask occupants whether their workspace supports concentration and interaction. Appleton's (1975) prospect-refuge theory and its successors (Hildebrand, 1991; Dosen & Ostwald, 2016) predict that occupants prefer locations affording broad visual prospect from a position of partial concealment, and that habitual occupants of refuge-deficient positions show measurable stress correlates. POE does not measure prospect-refuge.

**18. Wayfinding satisfaction versus cognitive-map accuracy (spatial, cognitive).** When buildings get wayfinding wrong, the standard POE item is "is the building easy to find your way around?" The literature distinguishes route knowledge, survey knowledge, and the genuinely deeper construct of cognitive-map accuracy, which can be measured by sketch-map drawing, pointing accuracy, and Euclidean error in estimated distances (Hölscher, Meilinger, Vrachliotis, Brösamle, & Knauff, 2006; Jeffery, 2019, present in our corpus). *Corpus presence: 38 evidence rows.*

**19. Space-syntax integration values versus the lived experience of legibility (spatial).** Space-syntax measures (Hillier & Hanson, 1984) are computed on the layout and predict global behavioural patterns reasonably well, but they say nothing about the local cues — landmarks, doorway visibility, colour and material continuity — that actually support route finding. Modern wayfinding research argues that the legibility variables are different from the integration variables (Hölscher et al., 2006). POE rarely measures either.

**20. Aesthetic preference versus neuroaesthetic response (spatial, neural).** Aesthetic preference is measured in POE by a single Likert item. The neuroaesthetic literature has shown reliable correlates in mPFC, OFC, and reward circuitry for stimuli rated as beautiful (Vartanian et al., 2013, the ceiling-height paper that has become canonical in built-environment neuroaesthetics; Coburn et al., 2020), and ceiling-height effects in particular interact with task type. *Corpus presence: 160 evidence rows on aesthetic-neural relations — one of our richest corpora.*

**21. Personal-control variables versus the perceived-control construct (control).** POE measures the *presence* of controls (thermostats, blinds, task lights) but rarely measures whether occupants believe the controls work or use them. The perceived-control literature (Skinner, 1996; Bordass, Leaman, & Willis, 1994) shows that the operative variable is the *belief* in efficacy, and that ineffective controls — controls present but disabled, or controls present but masked by central building-management overrides — are worse than absent controls for satisfaction.

**22. Self-reported productivity versus measured cognitive performance (cognitive, affect).** POE asks occupants whether they think the building helps or hurts their productivity. Self-reported productivity correlates only modestly with measured task performance (Tham, Sekhar, Cheong, & Wargocki, 2003). The literature has identified specific tasks — the Psychomotor Vigilance Task (Dinges & Powell, 1985), the Stroop, n-back working memory, and the sustained-attention-to-response task — that are sensitive to thermal, acoustic, and air-quality manipulations and that can be administered briefly in a POE setting. *Corpus presence: 36 evidence rows on cognitive performance measures; 5 specifically on PVT.*

**23. Comfort ratings versus stress physiology (affect, physio).** Self-report stress and comfort items are weakly correlated with the physiological-stress endpoints — cortisol, heart-rate variability, electrodermal activity — that predict longer-term health outcomes (Ulrich et al., 1991; Park, Tsunetsugu, Kasetani, Kagawa, & Miyazaki, 2010). *Corpus presence: 91 evidence rows on stress physiology; 47 on EDA; 52 on HRV; 21 on cortisol. This is a quantitatively strong area of our corpus.*

**24. Affective satisfaction versus restoration (affect).** Restoration is qualitatively different from positive affect: a restorative environment leaves attention replenished and directed-attention fatigue reversed, regardless of whether the occupant reports having enjoyed it. The Perceived Restorativeness Scale (Hartig, Korpela, Evans, & Gärling, 1997) operationalises this with subscales for being away, fascination, coherence, compatibility, and scope. *Corpus presence: 445 evidence rows touch restorative themes — easily our largest area.*

**25. Window presence versus daylight exposure timing (luminous, circadian, health).** A window is recorded; what is rarely recorded is the *temporal pattern* of daylight exposure across the working day, which is the relevant predictor for circadian entrainment, sleep quality, and longer-term mood outcomes (Boubekri, Cheung, Reid, Wang, & Zee, 2014). *Corpus presence: 28 evidence rows on luminous-wellbeing.*

**26. Indoor temperature versus skin-temperature gradient (thermal, physio).** Local skin-temperature gradients — distal-to-proximal — are better predictors of sleep-onset, alertness, and core-body thermoregulation than the building-air variables (Kräuchi & Wirz-Justice, 1994; Kompier, Smolders, van Marken Lichtenbelt, & de Kort, 2020, which is in our corpus). *Corpus presence: clear in our luminous-physio bundle.*

**27. Single-sense IEQ measures versus multisensory integration (multisensory).** Each of the IEQ domains is normally measured in isolation. The recent multisensory literature shows real interactions: thermal-acoustic, lighting-acoustic, and lighting-thermal pairs produce non-additive effects on perception and performance (Schweiker et al., 2020). *Corpus presence: 4 evidence rows in multisensory — a corpus gap.*

**28. Material specification versus material warmth and biophilia (material).** Materials are specified in POE by surface property — albedo, reflectance, U-value. The biophilic-design literature, much of it from our own university and our European collaborators (Browning, Ryan, & Clancy, 2014; Salingaros, 2015), shows that wood, fractal patterning, and natural materials produce measurable stress reduction in laboratory studies, independent of any thermal effect. *Corpus presence: limited but present — 12 evidence rows in material-physio.*

**29. View axis versus the prospect from preferred work positions (spatial).** Views are reported as "view from desk"; the literature shows that the preferred work position is one with prospect *and* refuge, and that occupants relocate when given the chance to do so (Maslow & Mintz, 1956 — the classic ugly-room study; Hildebrand, 1991).

**30. Occupant complaint counts versus latent dissatisfaction (general).** The single most damning POE finding from the BUS dataset is that complaint rates do not track underlying dissatisfaction: occupants in some cultures, age groups, and corporate climates suppress complaints; in others they amplify (Leaman & Bordass, 2007). A POE that measures complaints undercounts in the suppressive cases and over-counts in the expressive ones.

**31. Activity intensity proxies versus actual movement and circulation (behavioural).** Standard POE rarely measures behavioural patterns at all. The literature on stair use, activity intensity, and the influence of building layout on metabolic equivalents (Zimring, Joseph, Nicoll, & Tsepas, 2005) is well-established and shows substantial public-health stakes for layout choices.

**32. Workspace satisfaction versus territorial markers (psychological).** Vischer's psychological-comfort dimension picks up identity, belonging, and territoriality, none of which is captured in standard POE. The literature on workspace personalisation (Wells, 2000) shows that occupants who personalise their workspace report higher satisfaction and lower turnover intention.

**33. Generic occupant survey versus stratified-by-task survey (general).** A point about survey design rather than instrumentation: standard POE administers the same instrument to all occupants. The functional-comfort literature argues that the relevant evaluation depends on the task — a deep-focus task has different lighting and acoustic requirements than a collaborative task, and the same building can be functionally comfortable for one and uncomfortable for the other (Vischer, 2008).

**34. Static occupant survey versus longitudinal experience sampling (general, temporal).** Standard POE collects one snapshot per occupant; the experience-sampling tradition (Csikszentmihalyi & Larson, 1987, the foundational ESM paper; ≈4,000 citations) shows that occupant experience varies systematically by time of day, day of week, and task phase, and that a single-shot survey averages over variance that matters for diagnosis.

**35. Self-reported physical comfort versus implicit preference (general, implicit).** Self-report instruments require that the occupant introspect on a question and commit to a response. The implicit-cognition literature (Greenwald, McGhee, & Schwartz, 1998 — the foundational IAT paper; ≈40,000 citations) shows that occupants often hold implicit attitudes toward their workspace that diverge from their explicit reports, particularly for socially loaded variables (open-plan, hot-desking, glass-walled offices). The Nature-IAT (Schultz, Shriver, Tabanico, & Khazian, 2004) has been used in environmental psychology; an equivalent Built-Environment IAT could be a powerful POE instrument.

**36. Stated preference versus adaptive preference (general, philosophical-empirical).** Sen's (1985) notion of adaptive preference — that people who have lived under restricted conditions adapt their preferences downward to fit those conditions — has direct implications for POE: when occupants of a long-standing building report satisfaction, we cannot infer that the building is satisfying in any objective sense; they may have simply adjusted their expectations. The remedy is to elicit preferences against counterfactual descriptions of better conditions (a method drawn from welfare economics and from the capabilities literature; Nussbaum, 2000).

**37. Likert-scale comfort items versus Q-sort preference structure (general).** The Q-sort and the more recent 20-sort task elicit preference *structure* rather than position on a single dimension. Vischer's (2007) functional-comfort work explicitly used Q-sort in some studies; the wider POE tradition has not adopted it. *Corpus presence: 1 evidence row mentions Q-sort — a corpus gap and a methodological opportunity.*

**38. POE as building evaluation versus POE as building-and-occupant fit (general).** Almost everything in standard POE is framed as "is the building good?" A more useful framing is "for which occupants, doing which tasks, in which circumstances is this building good?", which is what the Vischer functional-comfort tradition and the fit-frame literature implicitly require but which the survey instruments do not implement.

That is thirty-eight; I have stopped where the marginal cost of finding another exceeds the marginal value of having one. Some of the items overlap somewhat (the acoustic items 1–4, for example, are not crisply independent), but I have kept them separate because each isolates a different measurement decision a POE practitioner could make. The pattern across the list is consistent: standard POE measures the *report* and rarely measures the *generator* of the report; standard POE measures the *physical aggregate* (lux, dBA, °C) and rarely measures the *human-relevant signal* (m-EDI, STI, MRT plus alliesthesia); standard POE measures the *snapshot* and rarely measures the *trajectory*.

## Part III — Methods for the Human-Centred Measurement of the Built Environment

The thirty-eight items above describe gaps in measurement. What follows is an inventory of the methods that can fill them. I have grouped these into five families, with attention to which tradition each draws on.

### Physiological measurement

The physiological methods are the strongest area of our corpus (over 200 evidence rows across cortisol, HRV, EDA, EEG, fNIRS, pupil, skin temperature). The methods in routine use are:

Salivary cortisol, measured by ELISA or LC-MS/MS, with a diurnal-baseline correction; sampled at fixed times of day, three to five times per day, across at least two days. Cortisol is the gold-standard physiological-stress endpoint for sustained occupational stress (Hellhammer, Wüst, & Kudielka, 2009) and is sensitive to environmental manipulations of light, noise, and view (Park et al., 2010).

Heart-rate variability, computed in the time domain (RMSSD, SDNN) and the frequency domain (LF/HF ratio) from a chest-strap or wrist-band ECG, providing an autonomic-balance index. HRV is a sensitive marker of acute stress, mental workload, and recovery (Thayer, Åhs, Fredrikson, Sollers, & Wager, 2012) and is now routine in environmental psychophysiology because the sensors have become inexpensive.

Electrodermal activity (also called skin conductance, EDA, or galvanic skin response), measured by two electrodes on the palm or wrist, sensitive to sympathetic arousal at a sub-second timescale. EDA is particularly useful in environments that produce phasic arousal — a startle, a glare event, a sudden noise — that the slower cardiovascular measures will average over (Boucsein, 2012).

Skin temperature, measured at multiple sites — distal (hand) and proximal (clavicle) — to derive the distal-to-proximal gradient, which is a strong predictor of sleep onset and a sensitive marker of thermoregulatory state (Kräuchi & Wirz-Justice, 1994).

EEG, ideally with mobile dry-electrode systems, providing alpha/theta-ratio and event-related-potential measures of attentional state. The frontal-alpha asymmetry literature ties this measure to approach-avoidance affect (Davidson, 1992). Our corpus contains 59 EEG-based studies.

fNIRS (functional near-infrared spectroscopy), measuring oxy- and deoxy-haemoglobin in cortical regions of interest, particularly the prefrontal cortex. fNIRS is portable enough for in-situ field studies and has been used in our corpus to compare neural responses to natural versus urban environments (see the Yin et al. studies indexed in the biophilia-physio bundle).

Pupillometry, both as a moment-to-moment arousal index and, in well-controlled lighting environments, as a measure of ipRGC activation through the post-illumination pupil response (PIPR; Kelbsch et al., 2019). PIPR is one of the few non-invasive measures of the melanopsin-driven non-visual response.

Eye-tracking, in fixed-position or head-mounted form, providing gaze paths, fixation durations, and saccade patterns. Useful for understanding what occupants actually look at in a space, which is itself a probe of cognitive-map construction and aesthetic processing.

### Psychometric self-report measurement

The CBE survey, the BUS survey, and the Soft Landings post-occupancy questionnaire dominate this space. Below these instruments sit a number of more specific scales:

The Positive and Negative Affect Schedule (PANAS; Watson, Clark, & Tellegen, 1988; ≈40,000 citations), a brief instrument for state affect.

The Self-Assessment Manikin (SAM; Bradley & Lang, 1994), a pictorial measure of valence, arousal, and dominance, useful when verbal items must be avoided (multi-lingual occupants, time-constrained settings).

The NASA Task-Load Index (NASA-TLX; Hart & Staveland, 1988), for workload assessment, particularly useful when one wants to separate functional-comfort decrements from raw-cognitive-demand decrements.

The Perceived Restorativeness Scale (Hartig et al., 1997; or the shorter PRS-11), for the restoration construct (item 24 above).

The Perceived Crowding Scale (Stokols, 1972), for the crowding construct (item 16 above).

The Place Attachment Scale (Williams & Vaske, 2003) and the Sense of Place inventories, for the territoriality and identity dimensions of Vischer's psychological-comfort construct.

The Connectedness to Nature Scale (Mayer & Frantz, 2004) for biophilic responsiveness, useful particularly in green-building POE.

The State-Trait Anxiety Inventory (Spielberger, 1983), routinely used in restoration studies in our corpus.

### Cognitive-performance measurement

The Psychomotor Vigilance Task (Dinges & Powell, 1985), a ten-minute sustained-attention task with very high test-retest reliability, used as the cognitive endpoint in the daylight, ventilation, and circadian studies in our corpus.

The Stroop task, n-back working-memory task, and Sustained Attention to Response Task (SART; Robertson, Manly, Andrade, Baddeley, & Yiend, 1997), each sensitive to different aspects of executive function and chosen according to the IEQ axis under investigation.

Verbal short-term memory tasks (immediate serial recall, reading-with-comprehension), which are the canonical task family used in the Hongisto STI work because they are most sensitive to the irrelevant-speech effect.

Creative-divergent-thinking tasks (the Alternate Uses Test; Guilford, 1967), useful when the building is meant to support innovation or design work.

### Implicit and preference-structure measurement

The Implicit Association Test (Greenwald, McGhee, & Schwartz, 1998), administered in environmental form. The Nature-IAT (Schultz et al., 2004) measures implicit nature-self association; a Built-Environment-IAT would measure implicit attitudes to specific spatial features.

Evaluative priming, an alternative implicit-measure paradigm with somewhat different validity properties (Fazio, Jackson, Dunton, & Williams, 1995).

Q-sort and 20-sort tasks, in which respondents sort a set of cards (statements, images of spaces) into a forced distribution from "most like me" to "least like me," yielding a preference structure rather than a single position. The 20-sort variant is a more constrained version useful for time-limited settings. Q-methodology (Stephenson, 1953; modern review McKeown & Thomas, 2013) is widely used in environmental-policy research and is well-suited to surfacing the diverse occupant perspectives that survey averages hide.

Choice-based conjoint analysis, in which respondents choose between bundled descriptions of hypothetical workspaces, allowing inference of the marginal utility of each environmental attribute (Louviere, Hensher, & Swait, 2000). Useful for design-brief calibration before a building is built and for evaluating renovation alternatives.

Adaptive-preference elicitation, in the Sen (1985, 1999) sense, in which respondents are asked first to evaluate their current conditions and then to evaluate counterfactual conditions; the comparison reveals how much the stated satisfaction depends on the occupant having normalised their current circumstance. This is a method drawn from welfare economics and the capabilities approach; it is rare in the architecture literature but worth importing for the reasons discussed under item 36 above.

### Ecological and behavioural measurement

The Experience Sampling Method (Csikszentmihalyi & Larson, 1987; modern review Hektner, Schmidt, & Csikszentmihalyi, 2007) and its clinical-research cousin Ecological Momentary Assessment, in which occupants receive multiple brief prompts per day on a smartphone, recording mood, location, activity, and environmental conditions at each prompt. The method captures the temporal trajectory that single-shot POE misses.

The Day Reconstruction Method (Kahneman, Krueger, Schkade, Schwarz, & Stone, 2004), a retrospective complement to ESM in which respondents reconstruct yesterday's day in episodes, rating each. The method is cheaper than ESM and produces complementary data.

Walking interviews (also called "go-along" interviews; Kusenbach, 2003; Carpiano, 2009), in which the researcher walks the building with the occupant and probes responses to specific spatial features in situ. The method captures local responses that decontextualised survey items will average over.

Behavioural-trace measurement via badge-based location tracking, occupancy sensors, and Wi-Fi access-point logs. Used carefully (and with explicit consent), these give the actual movement and circulation patterns that item 31 above identifies as a measurement gap.

Activity-recognition wearables, capturing posture, stair-vs-elevator use, and metabolic equivalents, with applications to the health-and-circulation literature (Zimring et al., 2005).

Annotated-photograph or video methods, in which occupants photograph aspects of the building that affect them positively or negatively and annotate them. The "Photovoice" tradition (Wang & Burris, 1997) is widely used in community-health research and translates well to POE.

The Office-Cognition-Function (COGfx) protocol (Allen et al., 2016), a packaged combination of cognitive-task batteries with environmental sensors, designed specifically for IEQ-cognition studies.

### A note on multi-method integration

The most defensible POE programmes combine at least three of these families: a physiological measure (HRV or EDA, as these are now inexpensive); a brief cognitive-performance task (PVT, four to ten minutes); a survey instrument (BUS or CBE for benchmarking, plus PRS or a domain-specific scale for the deeper construct of interest); and an ecological-temporal probe (ESM at minimum). The multi-method approach has the property — important in a contested-claim environment — that disagreements between channels are themselves diagnostic. When occupants self-report satisfaction but cortisol is elevated and PVT performance is degraded, the disagreement is itself a finding.

## Part IV — Toward a Presentation

If we make this into a talk, the natural arc is the one I have followed here: a brief opening on the history of POE that establishes the field's credibility; a long middle on the shallow-versus-deep contrast, in which each contrast is presented as a *worked example* (the room is the same, the measurement varies, the conclusion varies); and a closing methodological section that gives the audience the toolkit to do better. A standalone slide for each of the thirty-eight contrast cases is probably too many; my suggestion would be to pick eight or ten that span the IEQ domains and that have the most striking visual demonstrations available, and to bundle the remainder into an appendix or a one-slide "more cases" page that can be expanded in Q&A. The candidates with the strongest visualisations, in my view, are: STI/intelligibility of irrelevant speech (item 1, with audio demonstrations); m-EDI versus lux (item 5, with spectral plots and circadian-stimulus curves); daylight glare probability (item 7, with luminance images and the Wienold thresholds); adaptive thermal opportunity (item 10, with the de Dear-Brager comfort-vs-outdoor-temperature plot); CO₂ versus VOCs and PM (items 13–14, with the Allen COGfx headline numbers); view content versus view presence (item 8, with the Ulrich hospital-window result); prospect-refuge (item 17, with classic visual examples); aesthetic-neural correlates of ceiling height (item 20, the Vartanian plot is famously legible); and an explicit slide on adaptive preferences (item 36, because it punctures the satisfaction-survey premise of the entire field).

The audience-engagement payoff of the talk, I would argue, is the moment when the listener realises that a routine post-occupancy survey, conducted well by the standards of the field, can still systematically miss the variable that drives the experience the survey is supposed to capture. This is a genuinely interesting epistemological point — the standard instrument captures the layer of reportable phenomenal awareness and misses the layer of psychophysiological and behavioural response — and it is the point that distinguishes a Knowledge-Atlas-style review from a routine practitioner overview.
