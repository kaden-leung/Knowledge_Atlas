# Fifteen Experiences the Building Is Having on Its Occupants That Nobody Is Currently Measuring

*Talk outline — David Kirsh, draft 2026-05-19. Companion to `docs/POE_REVIEW_2026-05-19.md`.*

---

## Production notes

The talk is intended for an audience of architects, facilities people, and the more empirically minded among environmental designers, with the cognitive-science framing kept as a quiet undercurrent rather than the foreground. Nothing in the talk content refers to the Knowledge Atlas project, COGS 160, or the supporting corpus; those are infrastructure decisions, and an audience that does not need to hear about them does not need to hear about them. Where a slide invites a visual demonstration I have flagged it. The whole arc runs about forty minutes for the body plus ten or fifteen for the closing prescription, with Q&A on top.

The arc is: a brief opening that frames POE as a partly successful but constitutively shallow practice; the fifteen experiences, grouped loosely by IEQ axis but presented as fifteen first-person sentences; a closing methodological prescription — *three-channel evaluation* — that ties the talk together. The fifteen experiences are deliberately framed in the second person and the present tense, because that is the rhetorical register in which the audience will recognise themselves.

A note on the title: the verb "having on" is doing work. *Effect on*, *influence over*, *impact* — these are clinical. *Experience having on* invites the audience to consider that the building is the agent of a continuing action, and that the occupant is the locus of an unobserved response. This is a Russell-style choice; it works better in spoken English than the title's awkwardness on the page might suggest.

## Slide 1 — Title

> **Fifteen Experiences the Building Is Having on Its Occupants That Nobody Is Currently Measuring**

Subtitle: *A guide for architects, facilities managers, and anyone who has to defend a building after handover.*

Speaker note. Open with an admission. The post-occupancy evaluation is, after fifty years, a partly successful practice. We can now reliably tell a client whether occupants in their building are more or less satisfied than occupants of an average building of the same type. What we cannot tell them — and what the talk is about — is what is happening to those occupants that they themselves cannot report.

## Slide 2 — What POE typically asks

A small grid of the standard CBE / BUS items rendered in their actual fonts. "Are you satisfied with the temperature?" "Is the lighting glaring?" "Can you hear yourself think?" Seven domains, seven Likert scales, one summary score.

Speaker note. The standard instrument is good at what it does. It captures the layer of phenomenal awareness that the occupant can speak to. The argument of this talk is that there are at least fifteen experiences happening to that occupant, in that same building, that the instrument cannot reach.

## Slide 4 — Experience 1: Hearing your neighbour's intelligible conversation while trying to read

Layout: split-screen luminance image of an open-plan office on the left; a small waveform-and-STI value on the right; a bar showing reading-task performance against STI, with the Hongisto curve.

Body. Background speech at sixty decibels does not impair your reading task. Background speech at sixty decibels *that you can make out the words of* impairs your reading task by about ten per cent. The relevant physical variable is not loudness; it is the Speech Transmission Index — the intelligibility of the speech as it reaches your ear from across the room. Performance starts to fall when STI rises above about 0.20, drops sharply between 0.20 and 0.60, and plateaus beyond. The instrument cost is modest; the design implication — partitions and masking that target intelligibility rather than level — is large.

Cite: Hongisto (2005); Haka et al. (2009); Hongisto, Keränen, and Haapakangas (2020).

Demo suggestion. Audio clip — same speech at the same level, two STI conditions. The audience will hear it.

## Slide 5 — Experience 2: Your circadian system being told it is still night-time at ten in the morning

Layout: a spectral plot showing the daylight curve over a workday alongside the spectral output of typical office LEDs, with the melanopic-sensitivity curve overlaid; below, the m-EDI of each at the same horizontal lux.

Body. Standard lighting evaluation reports horizontal illuminance — three hundred lux at the desk, the LEED-credit number. The variable that drives your alertness, your daytime cortisol slope, and your nighttime sleep quality is not horizontal lux but melanopic Equivalent Daylight Illuminance — m-EDI — measured at the eye. The same lux value under a low-CCT LED can deliver a fraction of the circadian stimulus of the same lux under daylight. Recent consensus recommends a minimum of m-EDI 250 lux at the eye during the working day. Most offices are well below it, and nobody measures.

Cite: Lucas et al. (2014); CIE S 026 (2018); Brown et al. (2022).

Demo suggestion. A spectral plot is unfailingly compelling. The contrast between "this looks bright" and "your retina knows the difference" lands.

## Slide 6 — Experience 3: The micro-flinch when light hits the wrong part of your visual field

Layout: a fish-eye photograph of a daylit workstation with the DGP overlay rendered as a heat-map; below, the four Wienold thresholds (imperceptible / perceptible / disturbing / intolerable).

Body. Daylight is good, except when it is not. The metric that predicts whether a daylit room is actually comfortable is Daylight Glare Probability, computed from a fish-eye image and the vertical illuminance at the eye. The thresholds — under 35% imperceptible, over 45% intolerable — are within reach of any practice with a calibrated camera and a free piece of software. The reason DGP is rare in POE is that it is not in the survey, not that it is hard to measure. A high-daylight-autonomy room can be a high-glare room, and the green-rating systems will give the architect credit for one without penalising for the other.

Cite: Wienold and Christoffersen (2006); Wienold et al. (2019).

Demo suggestion. Two fish-eye images of the same desk — morning and afternoon — with DGP values 28% and 47%. The audience will recognise the second image as the one they would not sit at.

## Slide 7 — Experience 4: Looking out at concrete when your eyes need somewhere to rest

Layout: two views — the iconic Ulrich hospital photograph (or its modern equivalent) — one with vegetation, one with a brick wall. Below, the Ulrich result: 8.5% shorter stay, 22% less analgesic.

Body. Whether you have a view is the standard POE question. What the view is *of* — natural versus built, far versus near, vegetation versus pavement — is the variable that drives recovery time in hospitals, attention restoration in offices, and sleep quality at home. Ulrich's 1984 *Science* paper is one of the most-cited findings in environmental psychology; almost no post-occupancy evaluation operationalises it. The fix is a one-line addition to the survey — "What does the view contain?" — plus an annotated photo of the view as collected metadata.

Cite: Ulrich (1984); Kaplan (1995); Hartig, Mitchell, de Vries, and Frumkin (2014).

## Slide 8 — Experience 5: Wanting to open a window and not being able to

Layout: the de Dear and Brager adaptive-comfort plot — comfort temperature on the y-axis, outdoor mean temperature on the x-axis — with two clouds: naturally ventilated buildings tracking outdoor temperature in a wide band, sealed buildings clustered tightly at twenty-two degrees.

Body. The Fanger model says you are comfortable at twenty-two degrees in office clothes. The de Dear and Brager adaptive model — which is now in ASHRAE 55 — says that in a naturally ventilated building you are comfortable in a band several degrees wide, and that the band tracks the outdoor temperature. The variable that does the work is not the air temperature in the room but the *availability of adaptive opportunity*: a window you can open, a fan you can turn on, a dress code you can vary, a schedule you can flex. POE asks whether the temperature is in the comfort band. POE almost never asks whether the occupant has any of the affordances for adaptation.

Cite: de Dear and Brager (1998, 2002); Parkinson, de Dear, and Brager (2020).

---

## Slide 9 — Experience 6: Being cold on one side and warm on the other

Layout: a thermal image of an occupant at a desk by a single-glazed window in winter — the right hand visibly cooler than the left — with the ASHRAE 55 radiant-asymmetry limits annotated.

Body. The thermostat measures air temperature. Your body integrates over radiant temperature, especially in spaces with cold-window or warm-floor asymmetries. ASHRAE 55 sets limits — ten Kelvin head-to-foot for warm ceilings, five for cold ones — that are routinely violated in single-glazed envelopes and that no one measures. A globe thermometer is a five-hundred-dollar instrument. The mean radiant temperature, and especially its directional asymmetry, predicts comfort independently of the air temperature the thermostat sees.

Cite: Halawa, van Hoof, and Soebarto (2014); Parkinson, de Dear, and Brager (2020).

---

## Slide 10 — Experience 7: Reading your performance dip at two in the afternoon and not knowing why

Layout: a horizontal-bar chart of the Allen COGfx headline — cognitive function scores at conventional, green, and green-plus ventilation rates. The 61% headline is famous; the audience may have seen it. To its right, a stacked-bar of typical indoor concentrations of CO₂, total VOCs, and PM 2.5 with the COGfx green-plus condition as benchmark.

Body. CO₂ is easy to measure, so we measure CO₂. The Allen group's COGfx studies showed that doubling ventilation halves CO₂ *and* halves VOC concentration, and that cognitive performance scales with VOC reduction at levels well below current OSHA standards. The early-afternoon performance dip that occupants attribute to their lunch is more plausibly attributable to the build-up of bio-effluents and VOCs in a closed conference room. Routine POE does not measure VOCs or PM. The instruments are now under five thousand dollars and shrinking.

Cite: Allen et al. (2016); Wargocki, Wyon, Sundell, Clausen, and Fanger (2000); Power, Adar, Yanosky, and Weuve (2018).

---

## Slide 11 — Experience 8: Walking into a room that smells wrong

Layout: a small grid showing the three independent dimensions of olfactory air quality — intensity, hedonic tone, identifiability — with examples of buildings rated low on each.

Body. The standard air-quality survey item asks occupants to rate "freshness" on a five-point scale. The olfactory literature has known for forty years that perceived air quality is not one-dimensional. A building can have low measured pollutant levels and still smell wrong, because the *odour quality* — the hedonic tone, the identifiability of biological or chemical sources — signals contamination that the sensors do not pick up. Fanger's olf-and-decipol unit system is the operational instrument; the *Sniffer Bag* and panel-evaluation protocols are the field method.

Cite: Fanger (1988); Bluyssen (2014).

---

## Slide 12 — Experience 9: Being among too many strangers without any control over the encounter

Layout: a photograph of a crowded café (perceived as positive) and a photograph of a crowded open-plan office at the same physical density (perceived as negative). Below: the Stokols distinction.

Body. Crowding is not density. The same number of bodies in the same space can be experienced as restorative or stressful depending on whether the occupant has control over the encounter — whether they can leave, whether they know the others, whether the proximate strangers are predictable. Baum and Davis's long-corridor versus short-corridor dormitory study, in our literature since 1980, showed that the architectural intervention reduced perceived crowding while leaving density unchanged. POE measures the density. POE almost never measures the perceived control over proximity.

Cite: Stokols (1972); Baum and Davis (1980).

---

## Slide 13 — Experience 10: The instinct to sit with your back against the wall

Layout: a plan view of an open-plan office with the desk-occupancy heat-map overlaid: bright at the perimeter, dim in the central island desks. Below, the prospect-refuge diagram from Hildebrand.

Body. Appleton's prospect-refuge theory predicts that humans prefer locations affording broad visual prospect from a position of partial concealment. The empirical literature, from Hildebrand to Dosen and Ostwald's recent meta-analysis, has shown that occupants of refuge-deficient positions show measurable stress correlates and that occupants relocate when given the chance. Standard POE does not measure prospect-refuge balance. The data exists in any building with desk-occupancy sensors; the architects rarely see it.

Cite: Appleton (1975); Hildebrand (1991); Dosen and Ostwald (2016).

---

## Slide 14 — Experience 11: Not having a mental map of where you are

Layout: two side-by-side sketch maps of the same building, drawn from memory by two occupants — one accurate, one badly distorted. Below, the three levels of spatial knowledge: landmark, route, survey.

Body. "Is the building easy to find your way around?" is the standard POE wayfinding item. The cognitive-map literature distinguishes route knowledge from survey knowledge from the deeper construct of an accurate Euclidean cognitive map, which can be measured by sketch-map accuracy, pointing accuracy, and estimated-distance error. Buildings that violate space-syntax legibility — long corridors with branching, undifferentiated junctions, weak landmarks — produce occupants with route knowledge but no survey knowledge, who get lost in their own workplace months after moving in. POE does not measure cognitive-map accuracy. The sketch-map test is a five-minute administration.

Cite: Hölscher, Meilinger, Vrachliotis, Brösamle, and Knauff (2006); Jeffery (2019).

## Slide 15 — Experience 12: Looking up at a ceiling that does something to you

Layout: the Vartanian PNAS contour-and-ceiling-height figure showing the fMRI activation difference between high-ceilinged and low-ceilinged spaces, alongside the corresponding behavioural-approach data.

Body. Ceiling height affects you in a measurable way. So does contour — rounded versus rectilinear. So does the texture and material of the surfaces. The neuroaesthetic literature, much of it concentrated in the last fifteen years, has identified reliable activations in medial prefrontal cortex and orbital frontal cortex that distinguish the architecturally pleasant from the architecturally unpleasant, and these activations interact with the task the occupant is trying to perform — a low ceiling supports focused work, a high one supports divergent thinking. The standard POE rating of "spatial layout" averages over all of this. The asymmetric ceiling, the contour transitions, the material variation — these are not in the survey.

Cite: Vartanian et al. (2013); Coburn et al. (2020).

Demo suggestion. The Vartanian figure is one of the most legible images in environmental neuroscience. Use it.

---

## Slide 16 — Experience 13: Being told there is a thermostat but knowing it does nothing

Layout: a photograph of a wall thermostat with the central-BMS override icon visible, alongside the Leaman-Bordass "killer variables" figure.

Body. The standard POE item asks whether the occupant has access to controls. The Leaman and Bordass analysis of the BUS dataset showed that the largest single predictor of building satisfaction is personal control over thermal, lighting, and acoustic conditions, eclipsing the variance attributable to whether the absolute environmental measurements meet code. The further finding — perhaps more interesting — is that the operative variable is *belief in efficacy*. A thermostat that the occupant believes does not work is worse than no thermostat at all, because it generates a learned helplessness about the ambient environment. POE measures the presence of controls. It almost never measures the perceived efficacy of those controls.

Cite: Leaman and Bordass (1999); Skinner (1996); Bordass, Leaman, and Willis (1994).

---

## Slide 17 — Experience 14: Coming back from lunch and still feeling tired

Layout: the Hartig PRS factor structure on the left — being away, fascination, coherence, compatibility, scope. On the right, a plot of pre-and-post directed-attention task performance in a restorative versus a non-restorative break environment.

Body. Restoration is not the same as positive affect. A restorative environment leaves directed attention replenished and the mental-fatigue indicators reversed, whether or not the occupant reports enjoying the experience. The Perceived Restorativeness Scale operationalises this with five factors, and the Necker-cube reversal task, the digit-span backwards task, and the attention-network task are the cognitive endpoints. Most offices, even ones designed for biophilic effect, have no restorative space; the lunch break occurs at the same desk. POE measures satisfaction with the break facilities. It does not measure whether the occupant has anywhere to actually restore.

Cite: Hartig, Korpela, Evans, and Gärling (1997); Kaplan (1995).

## Slide 18 — Experience 15: Saying you are satisfied with what you no longer remember could be different

Layout: a quotation slide. Amartya Sen, from *Commodities and Capabilities*, on adaptive preference: "The destitute thrown into starvation can accept the deprivation as part of his nature." Below, an empty Likert scale.

Body. The deepest problem with the satisfaction survey is that satisfaction can adapt. Occupants of a long-standing building have normalised their conditions; their stated satisfaction tells us as much about their reference frame as it does about the building. Sen's notion of adaptive preference — that people who have lived under restricted conditions adjust their preferences to fit those conditions — has direct implications for POE: the only way to elicit non-adapted preferences is to ask against counterfactuals. *Imagine an office with an openable window. How would you rate the air?* *Imagine an office with a sketch-mapable layout. How would you rate the wayfinding?* The counterfactual elicitation is a method drawn from welfare economics; it is rare in the architecture literature, and it puts the conclusions of the satisfaction survey on a much firmer footing.

Cite: Sen (1985, 1999); Nussbaum (2000).

Speaker note. This is the slide that lands the hardest. It says that the survey instrument the audience has been buying for thirty years has a structural blind spot, and it offers the philosophical literature that has been thinking about exactly this blind spot for forty years. The slide is the bridge from the catalogue of fifteen to the prescription that follows.

## Slide 19 — The pattern

A three-line summary slide.

> The standard instrument measures the *report* and rarely the *generator*.
>
> The standard instrument measures the *physical aggregate* and rarely the *human-relevant signal*.
>
> The standard instrument measures the *snapshot* and rarely the *trajectory*.

Speaker note. Each of the fifteen experiences exemplifies at least one of these three failure modes. The diagnosis is not that POE is doing the wrong thing; it is that POE is restricted to one of three channels of evidence available to it, and as a result the instrument is structurally shallow.

## Slide 20 — The prescription: three-channel evaluation

A diagram. Three vertical channels.

- **Channel one — self-report.** BUS, CBE, PRS, NASA-TLX, single-item productivity, the satisfaction survey we already have. *What the occupant can tell us.*
- **Channel two — physiology.** Heart-rate variability, electrodermal activity, salivary cortisol, skin-temperature gradient, optionally EEG or fNIRS at higher-end installations. *What the occupant cannot consciously report.*
- **Channel three — implicit.** Implicit Association Test, evaluative priming, Q-sort or 20-sort preference structure, choice-based conjoint, adaptive-preference elicitation against counterfactuals. *What the occupant does not know they prefer.*

Speaker note. The argument of the talk is that no POE programme that aspires to be more than satisfaction-tracking can rest on the self-report channel alone. The physiological channel reaches the body's response to the environment, which is the response that drives long-term health and which the occupant cannot introspect on. The implicit channel reaches the preferences underneath the verbal report, which are the preferences that survive adaptation and that predict choice-under-information. A serious POE picks one instrument from each channel.

---

## Slide 21 — A minimum kit

A practical slide. The audience wants to leave with something they can ask their facilities team for on Monday.

- Self-report: the CBE Occupant Survey, with an added PRS-11 module and a counterfactual elicitation block.
- Physiology: a one-week field study with chest-strap HRV monitors and salivary cortisol samples three times a day, run on a stratified sample of twenty to fifty occupants.
- Implicit: a Built-Environment-IAT, administered once at six months and once at twenty-four months post-occupancy, paired with a Q-sort of fifty workspace-feature cards.

Cost. Under twenty thousand dollars for a fifty-person sample, run once. Cheaper than the energy audit that the same buildings already do.

## Slide 22 — Why this matters — the performance-gap argument

A simple bar chart: predicted versus measured energy use in LEED-certified buildings, the Newsham 2009 result.

Body. The energy performance-gap literature has institutionalised the practice of comparing predicted to measured for buildings. The human-experience performance-gap is much larger, because the predicted satisfaction in the architect's brief almost never gets compared with the achieved satisfaction in the occupant's response. Three-channel POE is the only mechanism I know of that closes the loop on the human side with the same rigour the energy side now expects.

Cite: Newsham, Mancini, and Birt (2009); Frankel and Turner (2008).

## Slide 23 — What comes after the talk

A short, optimistic slide. The next ten years of POE will look like the last ten years of clinical-trials methodology: multi-channel, multi-time-point, sensitive to mechanism, and with a clear distinction between the verbal report (whose blind spots we now know) and the physiological-and-implicit measures (whose blind spots are different from the verbal report's, and whose disagreements are themselves informative).

A POE programme that disagrees with itself across channels has *found something*. A POE programme that agrees with itself across channels has *confirmed something*. Either outcome is a result.

## Notes for the speaker

The fifteen-experience structure puts substantial cognitive load on the audience; some will tune out around experience seven or eight. Two ways to counter this. First, the visual demonstrations have to be strong: the audio clip at experience one, the spectral plot at experience two, the fish-eye DGP at experience three, the de Dear-Brager plot at experience five, the Allen COGfx headline at experience seven, the Vartanian fMRI at experience twelve. Second, the rhetorical shift at slide nineteen — the three-line pattern — has to land as a genuine *aha*. If it does, the prescription on slide twenty has a place to go. If it does not, the talk ends as a catalogue rather than as an argument.

The single phrase to come back to throughout: *the building is doing something to its occupants, and the standard instrument cannot see it.*
